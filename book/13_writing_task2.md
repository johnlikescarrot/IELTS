# Chapter 13: Writing Task 2 — Essays

## 13.1 Task 2 Essentials

Answer the question in **250+ words (aim 260–290) within 40 minutes**. Worth **2/3 of your Writing band** — the single highest-leverage 40 minutes in IELTS. Five question types (below); each needs its own structure. A clear, sustained **position** is mandatory: fence-sitting or self-contradiction caps Task Response at Band 5–6.

**The universal 4-paragraph essay (Introduction – Body 1 – Body 2 – Conclusion):**

1. **Introduction:** paraphrase the question (1–2 sentences) + thesis/position (1 sentence) + outline (optional 1 sentence).
2. **Body 1:** topic sentence → explanation → example → (link back).
3. **Body 2:** topic sentence → explanation → example → (link back).
4. **Conclusion:** restate position + summarise main points (no new ideas).

> Video Parts 10–12 build Task 2 exactly this way: scoring wheel (Task Response version) → introductions with thesis statements → body paragraphs (B1, B2) → conclusions → full essays, with grammar (concession/reason linkers) woven throughout.

## 13.2 Reading the Question (2 minutes that decide your band)

Underline **topic words** (subject) and **instruction words** (task). Five types:

| # | Type | Instruction words | Structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opinion (agree/disagree) | *To what extent do you agree? Do you agree or disagree?* | Intro (position: agree/disagree/balanced) → B1 (your main side) → B2 (your main side OR concession + refutation) → Conclusion |
| 2 | Discussion (both views + opinion) | *Discuss both views and give your own opinion.* | Intro → B1 (view A fairly) → B2 (view B fairly + your opinion clear) → Conclusion (opinion restated) |
| 3 | Advantages/disadvantages | *Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages? / Discuss...* | Intro → B1 (advantages) → B2 (disadvantages + judgment) → Conclusion (outweigh verdict) |
| 4 | Problem/solution (+cause) | *What are the causes? What can be done? / Problems and solutions* | Intro → B1 (causes/problems) → B2 (solutions, matched 1:1 to causes) → Conclusion |
| 5 | Two-part/direct question | *Why...? Is this positive or negative? / What...? How...?* | Intro → B1 (answers Q1) → B2 (answers Q2) → Conclusion |

! Type errors are fatal: discussing both views when asked only your opinion (or vice versa), listing advantages without an outweigh-verdict, giving solutions that don't match your stated causes. Name the type before you plan.

## 13.3 Introductions: Paraphrase + Thesis

**Sentence 1–2 (background/paraphrase):** restate the topic with synonyms and changed grammar. The course's rule: never copy question words — *famous → prominent/renowned; obstacles → hurdles.*

- Q: *Advances in technology and automation have reduced the need for manual labour.*
- P: *Technological progress and automation have significantly diminished demand for physical work.*

**Sentence 3 (thesis/position):** answer the instruction directly.

- Opinion: *In my view, working hours should indeed be reduced, though with important conditions.* (clear position + hint of concession)
- Discussion: *This essay will examine both perspectives before arguing that...*
- Outweigh: *Although there are drawbacks, I believe the advantages are more significant.*
- Problem/solution: *This essay will analyse the main causes and propose effective measures.*

**Course rules for introductions (Part 10):** general statements + thesis statement; full sentences; never memorised openers (*"Nowadays, with the development of society..."* — examiners groan); never contractions; leave a blank line between paragraphs (or indent — one system, consistently).

## 13.4 Body Paragraphs: PEEL + Concession

**PEEL:** Point (topic sentence) → Explain (2–3 sentences: why/how) → Example (specific: country, study, number, personal observation) → Link (tie back to the question).

>>> Body paragraph model (opinion essay, automation): "The strongest argument for shorter hours is that productivity no longer depends on time spent at desks. Since software now handles routine administration, data entry, and even basic analysis, employees produce the same output in fewer hours. For example, Microsoft Japan's 2019 four-day-week trial raised productivity by 40% while cutting costs. This demonstrates that reducing hours, far from harming the economy, can make it more efficient." (Point → explanation with since-clause → specific example → link. 78 words — the ideal body paragraph length is 70–90 words.)

**Concession + refutation (the Band 7+ move):** devote 2–3 sentences of Body 2 to the other side, then defeat it: *"Critics argue that shorter hours would cut pay for hourly workers. While this risk is real, it can be addressed through minimum-wage protection and output-based pay; indeed, trials show..."* — concession (While...) + solution + evidence.

## 13.5 Conclusions: Restate, Don't Repeat

2–3 sentences: position restated (new words) + main reasons summarised + (optional) forward-looking final line. No new arguments, no new examples, no "In a nutshell".

- Weak: *"In conclusion, I agree with this. There are good points on both sides."* (vague, no summary)
- Strong: *"In conclusion, I largely agree that automation justifies shorter working hours, since output has decoupled from time spent working. Provided wages are safeguarded, reduced hours promise both economic efficiency and human wellbeing."*

## 13.6 FULL WORKED ESSAY — Automation & Working Hours (Video Part 12)

**Question:** *Advances in technology and automation have reduced the need for manual labour. Therefore, working hours should be reduced. To what extent do you agree?* (Type 1: opinion.)

**Model essay (278 words, Band 8–9):**

*Technological progress and automation have significantly diminished demand for physical work. As a result, some argue that the standard working week should be shortened. In my view, working hours should indeed be reduced, though only alongside measures to protect workers' incomes.*

*The strongest argument for shorter hours is that productivity no longer depends on time spent at desks. Since software now handles routine administration, data entry, and even basic analysis, employees produce the same output in fewer hours. For example, Microsoft Japan's 2019 four-day-week trial raised productivity by 40% while cutting electricity costs by nearly a quarter. This demonstrates that reducing hours, far from harming the economy, can make it more efficient.*

*Furthermore, shorter weeks would address the growing crisis of overwork. Chronic stress and burnout now cost economies billions in healthcare and lost output; giving people genuine leisure time would improve both mental health and, paradoxically, the quality of the hours actually worked. Countries experimenting with reduced hours, such as Iceland, have reported stable or improved service levels alongside markedly better wellbeing.*

*Admittedly, critics warn that hourly-paid workers could lose income if hours fall. While this danger is genuine, it reflects poor policy design rather than any flaw in the principle: minimum-hours guarantees and output-linked pay can preserve earnings, as several trial schemes have shown.*

*In conclusion, I largely agree that automation justifies shorter working hours, since economic output has decoupled from time spent working. Provided wages are safeguarded, reduced hours promise a rare win-win: greater efficiency for economies and healthier lives for workers.*

**Examiner-style annotation:**

- **TR:** position clear throughout (agree + condition); all parts answered (automation premise accepted, hours verdict given); ideas extended with examples; 278 words.
- **CC:** 5 paragraphs (intro, B1, B2, concession, conclusion); progression clear; linkers varied (*as a result, since, for example, furthermore, admittedly, while, provided*); reference (*this demonstrates, this danger, the principle*).
- **LR:** diminished demand, decoupled, chronic stress, burnout, paradoxically, markedly, win-win; collocations (*raise productivity, preserve earnings, safeguard wages*); no copied chunks.
- **GRA:** complex throughout (*Since software now handles..., ...while cutting...; Provided wages are safeguarded...*); concession frames (*Admittedly... While...*); passive (*are safeguarded*); conditional nuance; frequent error-free sentences.

## 13.7 Four More Complete Model Essays (abbreviated with skeletons)

**Essay B — Discussion (Type 2):** *Some people think university education should be free for all students. Others believe students should pay for their own tuition. Discuss both views and give your opinion.* (272 words.) B1 (free): equality of opportunity — talent over wealth; example: Germany's free universities + social mobility data. B2 (pay): taxpayer burden + resource waste; BUT opinion: means-tested fees (fair + sustainable). Conclusion: free-at-point-of-use funded progressively. *Key phrases: proponents contend / opponents maintain; while I acknowledge... I believe...; a middle path...*

**Essay C — Outweigh (Type 3):** *In many countries, people now wear western-style clothing. Do the advantages of this outweigh the disadvantages?* (265 words.) Advantages: practicality, global business, affordability. Disadvantages: cultural erosion, lost craftsmanship. Verdict: advantages outweigh *provided* heritage dress is preserved ceremonially — concession with condition (the classic outweigh structure). *Key phrases: chief benefit / principal drawback; on balance; outweigh.*

**Essay D — Problem/solution (Type 4):** *Traffic congestion in major cities is worsening. What are the causes, and what measures can governments take?* (281 words.) Causes: car ownership + poor public transport + urban sprawl (pick TWO, develop fully). Solutions matched 1:1: congestion charging + mass-transit investment. *Key phrases: The root causes are twofold; To address the first...; Turning to the second...; Such measures have proven effective in... (Singapore, London).*

**Essay E — Two-part (Type 5):** *Many young people leave their hometowns for cities. Why? Is this a positive or negative development?* (269 words.) Why: jobs + education + lifestyle (push/pull). Judgment: mixed, but overall negative for rural communities (ageing, skills drain) while positive for individuals — nuanced verdicts score TR highly when clearly stated. *Key phrases: driven by...; pull factors; while individuals benefit, communities...; on balance.*

> Write each skeleton into a full essay yourself, then compare with the automation model for structure, linker variety, and example specificity. Rewriting > reading.

## 13.8 Cohesion in Action — Linker Placement Map

One essay's linker budget (the automation model uses 12 devices — count them): intro (1–2: *as a result*), B1 (3: *since, for example, this demonstrates*), B2 (2: *furthermore, alongside*), concession (3: *admittedly, while, as*), conclusion (2: *since, provided*). Notice: **no linker repeated**, reference chains (*this, such, the principle*) carry half the cohesion invisibly, and paragraph-first linkers (*Furthermore, Admittedly, In conclusion*) signpost the argument's turns.

## 13.9 The 5 Fatal Task 2 Errors (and fixes)

1. **No clear position** → Write your thesis before the essay; echo it in every body paragraph's link sentence.
2. **Memorised chunks** (*"a controversial hot topic in today's society"*) → Delete all memorised openers; paraphrase the actual question.
3. **Ideas without examples** → Every body paragraph needs one specific: country, company, study, figure, or observed fact.
4. **One-side-only discussion** (Type 2 without both views) → B1 = other side (fairly!), B2 = your side + verdict.
5. **New ideas in conclusion** → Conclusion = restate + summarise only. New idea? It belongs in a body paragraph — restructure.

## 13.10 TWO MORE FULL MODEL ESSAYS

**Essay F — Discussion, full (Type 2, 274 words):** *Some people think university education should be free for all students. Others believe students should pay for their own tuition. Discuss both views and give your opinion.*

*Whether higher education should be free is one of the most debated questions in education policy. While some argue that tuition fees are necessary, others insist that universities should charge nothing. This essay will examine both perspectives before concluding that a means-tested system is fairest.*

*Proponents of free university argue principally from equality of opportunity. If tuition is free, the brightest students attend regardless of family wealth, allowing societies to develop all available talent. Germany, which abolished tuition fees in most states, enrols a higher proportion of working-class students than England, where fees exceed £9,000 per year. Supporters also note the public return: graduates pay more tax over their lifetimes, arguably repaying the investment.*

*Opponents, however, question both the fairness and the efficiency of universal free tuition. They point out that graduates earn substantially more than non-graduates, so free university effectively taxes cleaners to subsidise future lawyers. Moreover, when education is free, some students enrol without commitment, wasting places and resources — a problem visible in countries with high dropout rates.*

*In my view, the fairest solution combines both insights: tuition should be free at the point of use but repaid progressively through the tax system once graduates earn above a threshold. This preserves open access while ensuring beneficiaries contribute. Such income-linked systems already work well in Australia.*

*In conclusion, while free access promotes fairness and paid tuition promotes efficiency, neither extreme is optimal. A means-tested model delivers the benefits of both.*

**Essay G — Problem/solution, full (Type 4, 283 words):** *Traffic congestion in major cities is worsening. What are the causes, and what measures can governments take to tackle it?*

*Gridlock now blights most major cities, wasting time, fuel, and lives. This essay will analyse the two root causes of worsening congestion and propose matched measures for each.*

*The first cause is the sheer growth in private car ownership, driven by rising incomes and cheap credit. In Bangkok, vehicle numbers have tripled in twenty years while road space has grown barely 10%. Compounding this, public transport in many cities is infrequent, uncomfortable, or unsafe, leaving commuters with little alternative to driving. When buses crawl in the same jams as cars, switching modes offers no advantage.*

*To address car dependency, governments should introduce congestion charging: a daily fee for driving into city centres. London's charge cut traffic by 21% within a year, and Singapore's electronic pricing has kept its roads flowing for decades. Revenue should be ring-fenced for the second measure — mass-transit investment: metro lines, bus lanes physically separated from traffic, and integrated ticketing. Experience from Seoul shows that when public transport is faster than driving, commuters switch voluntarily.*

*Admittedly, both measures face opposition: charging is called unfair to the poor, and metros are expensive. Yet fairness can be protected through exemptions and improved buses serving outer districts first, while metro costs are dwarfed by the economic losses congestion already inflicts.*

*In conclusion, congestion stems from car growth outpacing both roads and transit. Congestion charging plus mass-transit investment — proven from London to Seoul — can reverse it.*

## 13.11 Introduction & Conclusion Banks (adapt, never memorise)

**Paraphrase pairs (question → introduction):** *have reduced the need → have diminished demand for; manual labour → physical work; should be reduced → ought to be cut / it is argued hours should fall; major cities → large urban centres / metropolitan areas; is worsening → has deteriorated / continues to intensify; university education → tertiary education / higher education; should be free → should charge no fees / ought to be publicly funded; some people think → it is often argued / many contend; others believe → opponents maintain / others take the opposite view; the best way → the most effective means; the main cause → the primary driver.*

**Thesis frames by type:** Opinion: *In my view... though with important qualifications. / I largely agree that... / I disagree, for reasons explained below.* Discussion: *This essay will examine both perspectives before arguing that...* Outweigh: *Although there are genuine drawbacks, I believe the benefits are more significant.* Problem/solution: *This essay will analyse the principal causes and propose effective responses.* Two-part: *This essay will first explain... before assessing whether...*

**Conclusion frames:** *In conclusion, I (largely/strongly) agree that... since/because... / Taking everything into account, ... outweigh(s)... / ...stems from...; ...can address/reverse/resolve it. / While... has merits, ... remains the stronger position because...* + optional forward line: *Provided... is/are safeguarded/addressed, ... promises...*
