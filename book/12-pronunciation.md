# Chapter 11 — Pronunciation
^ Twenty-five per cent of the Speaking band, and the one most candidates never train

The course devotes substantial time to pronunciation, and frames it precisely: pronunciation means producing words in a way that does not create misunderstanding. As the teacher puts it, mispronunciation "may cause some kind of misunderstanding at the listener's end, which may reduce your band score."

Two things follow. First, pronunciation is trainable — it is a motor skill, not a talent. Second, the target is **intelligibility**, not an accent.

## 11.1 Where pronunciation problems come from

The course identifies two sources, and both are worth taking seriously.

:::key
**Source 1: You learned the word wrongly.**
If you first encountered a word in writing and guessed its pronunciation, or heard it mispronounced by a teacher or friend, the error fossilises. The teacher describes a friend who learned one word incorrectly years ago and has said it that way ever since.
**The fix:** never learn a new word without hearing it. Every dictionary app has audio. Use it every single time, including for words you are sure about.
**Source 2: Interference from your first language.**
Your mother tongue's sound system filters English. Sounds that don't exist in your L1 get replaced by the nearest one you have; stress patterns transfer; syllable timing transfers.
**The fix:** identify the specific sounds your language lacks and drill those, rather than practising "pronunciation" in general.
:::

## 11.2 Minimal pairs: the sounds that carry meaning

A minimal pair is two words differing by one sound. If you cannot distinguish them, you cannot be reliably understood.

The course drills **/p/ versus /b/** at length, and the choice is instructive: these are the same in every way except **voicing** (whether the vocal cords vibrate) and **aspiration** (the puff of air).

### /p/ and /b/

| /p/ | /b/ |
| --- | --- |
| pay | bay |
| pin | bin |
| pear | bear |
| cap | cab |
| rope | robe |
| pest | best |
| Pepsi | — |

**How to feel the difference:** hold a sheet of paper an inch from your mouth. Say *pin* — the paper should move sharply. Say *bin* — it should barely move. Then put a finger on your throat: /b/ vibrates, /p/ does not.

### Other high-frequency minimal pairs

| Contrast | Examples | Who struggles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **/f/ – /v/** | fan/van, safe/save, leaf/leave | German, Hindi, Arabic |
| **/b/ – /v/** | boat/vote, berry/very, ban/van | Spanish, Arabic, Korean |
| **/p/ – /f/** | pan/fan, pool/fool | Japanese, Korean |
| **/l/ – /r/** | light/right, collect/correct, glass/grass | Japanese, Korean, Chinese |
| **/s/ – /ʃ/** | sea/she, sock/shock | Greek, some Chinese |
| **/θ/ – /s/ – /t/** | think/sink, three/tree, path/pass | very widespread |
| **/ð/ – /d/ – /z/** | they/day, breathe/breed | very widespread |
| **/n/ – /ŋ/** | sin/sing, ban/bang | Spanish, Italian |
| **/ɪ/ – /iː/** | ship/sheep, bit/beat, live/leave | Spanish, Arabic, Japanese |
| **/æ/ – /e/** | bad/bed, man/men, sat/set | many |
| **/ʌ/ – /ɑː/** | cup/carp, but/bart | many |
| **/ɒ/ – /əʊ/** | not/note, cost/coast | many |
| **/w/ – /v/** | west/vest, wine/vine | German, Indian languages |

### The /θ/ and /ð/ sounds

*th* has two pronunciations and neither exists in most languages.

- **Voiceless /θ/** — *think, three, path, month, both, through, healthy*
- **Voiced /ð/** — *this, that, they, the, mother, breathe, either, weather*

**Technique:** tongue tip lightly between the teeth, blow air. For /ð/, add voice. It feels absurd; it is correct. Substituting /s/ or /t/ is the single most noticeable non-native feature in English and it does occasionally cause genuine confusion (*thin* / *sin*, *three* / *tree*).

## 11.4 Word stress

English is a **stress-timed** language. Every multi-syllable word has one strongly stressed syllable, and getting it wrong can make the word unrecognisable even when every sound is correct.

:::warn
**Misplaced stress is more damaging than a wrong vowel.**
*PHOtograph / phoTOgrapher / photoGRAPHic* — same root, three different stress patterns, and the unstressed vowels reduce to /ə/ in each. A listener hearing *photoGRAPH* instead of *PHOtograph* may not recognise the word at all.
:::

### Stress rules that actually work

| Rule | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Two-syllable nouns/adjectives:** usually stress 1 | **TA**ble, **HAP**py, **DOC**tor, **MOU**ntain |
| **Two-syllable verbs:** usually stress 2 | be**GIN**, de**CIDE**, re**LAX**, for**GET** |
| **Noun/verb pairs:** noun stress 1, verb stress 2 | **RE**cord (n) / re**CORD** (v); **PRE**sent / pre**SENT**; **IN**crease / in**CREASE**; **CON**duct / con**DUCT**; **EX**port / ex**PORT** |
| **-tion, -sion, -cian:** stress the syllable before | edu**CA**tion, deci**SION**, musi**CIAN**, informa**TION** |
| **-ity, -ify, -ical, -ular:** stress the syllable before | a**BI**lity, i**DEN**tify, e**CO**nomical, par**TI**cular |
| **-ic:** stress the syllable before | scien**TI**fic, ath**LE**tic, eco**NO**mic |
| **-ate (verbs):** stress two back | **GRA**duate (v), **E**ducate, com**MU**nicate |
| **-ee, -eer, -ese, -ette:** stress the suffix | employ**EE**, engin**EER**, Japan**ESE**, ciga**RETTE** |
| **Compound nouns:** stress the first element | **BLACK**board, **AIR**port, **POST**man, **GREEN**house |
| **Compound adjectives:** stress the second | old-**FASH**ioned, well-**KNOWN**, bad-**TEM**pered |
| **Phrasal verbs:** stress the particle | give **UP**, look **AF**ter, put **OFF** |

### Words IELTS candidates commonly misstress

*de**VE**lop, **COM**fortable, **VE**getable, **NE**cessary, **IN**teresting, **TE**mperature, cate**GO**rical, **PHO**tograph, **CO**lleague, **RE**staurant, de**TER**mine, **CHA**racter, **MA**nagement, a**VAI**lable, de**VE**lopment, en**VI**ronment, ad**VER**tisement (BrE), **PRE**ference, com**PA**rison, ana**LY**sis, hy**PO**thesis, phe**NO**menon*

## 11.5 Sentence stress and rhythm

Within a sentence, **content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words) are stressed. **Function words** (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns, conjunctions) are unstressed and reduced.

> *I **WENT** to the **SHOP** to **BUY** some **BREAD**.*

Four stressed syllables. Everything between them is compressed. English rhythm places stresses at roughly equal intervals regardless of how many unstressed syllables sit between them — which is why *"CATS EAT FISH"* and *"the CATS have been EATing the FISH"* take about the same time to say.

:::key
**This is why English sounds "fast".**
It is not faster. It compresses unstressed syllables. If you give every syllable equal weight — natural if your first language is syllable-timed, like Spanish, French, Italian, Japanese, Turkish or Arabic — you will be understood, but you will sound effortful, and Fluency and Pronunciation both suffer.
**Drill:** clap a steady beat. Say a sentence with one stressed syllable per clap, cramming the unstressed syllables in between.
:::

### Weak forms

Function words have reduced pronunciations in connected speech. Learning them improves your listening as much as your speaking.

| Word | Strong | Weak |
| --- | --- | --- |
| and | /ænd/ | /ən/, /n/ — *fish 'n' chips* |
| to | /tuː/ | /tə/ — *I want tə go* |
| of | /ɒv/ | /əv/, /ə/ — *a cup ə tea* |
| for | /fɔː/ | /fə/ |
| can | /kæn/ | /kən/ |
| are | /ɑː/ | /ə/ |
| have | /hæv/ | /əv/, /v/ |
| was | /wɒz/ | /wəz/ |
| them | /ðem/ | /ðəm/, /əm/ |
| you | /juː/ | /jə/ |

**Contrastive stress** — stressing a word to change meaning:
> *I didn't say **SHE** stole it.* (someone else did)
> *I didn't say she **STOLE** it.* (she borrowed it)
> *I didn't **SAY** she stole it.* (I implied it)

Using contrastive stress deliberately is a strong Band 8 pronunciation feature.

## 11.6 Connected speech

Native speakers do not pronounce words in isolation. Four processes:

| Process | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Linking** | Final consonant joins to initial vowel | *an apple* → "a-napple"; *turn off* → "tur-noff" |
| **Intrusion** | /w/, /j/ or /r/ inserted between vowels | *go on* → "go-**w**-on"; *I am* → "I-**y**-am"; *law and order* (BrE) → "law-**r**-and" |
| **Elision** | A sound disappears | *next day* → "nex' day"; *friendship* → "frien'ship" |
| **Assimilation** | A sound changes to match its neighbour | *ten boys* → "tem boys"; *would you* → "wouldjou" |

You do not have to produce these to score well, but you **must recognise them** to succeed in Listening. Practise by transcribing short stretches of natural speech (Chapter 2.11).

## 11.7 Intonation

The melody of speech. Wrong intonation makes correct grammar sound rude, bored or confused.

| Pattern | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Falling** ↘ | Statements, wh-questions, commands, finished lists | *I live in Cairo.* ↘ / *Where do you live?* ↘ |
| **Rising** ↗ | Yes/no questions, uncertainty, unfinished lists | *Do you like it?* ↗ / *I bought bread* ↗*, milk* ↗*, and eggs* ↘ |
| **Fall-rise** ↘↗ | Reservation, politeness, implied "but" | *The food was nice...* ↘↗ (but something wasn't) |
| **Rise-fall** ↗↘ | Strong feeling, surprise | *That's amazing!* ↗↘ |

:::warn
**Flat intonation is the commonest pronunciation problem at Band 6.**
Monotone delivery reads as disengagement even when the content is excellent. It also loses you the "range of pronunciation features" credit at Band 7 and above. Vary your pitch deliberately: go noticeably higher on the stressed syllable of the most important word in each sentence.
:::

## 11.8 British and American differences

The course covers these briefly and correctly frames them as **awareness**, not a target.

| Feature | British (RP) | American (GA) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **/r/ after a vowel** | dropped — *car* /kɑː/, *park* /pɑːk/ | pronounced — /kɑːr/, /pɑːrk/ |
| **/t/ between vowels** | clear /t/ — *water* /ˈwɔːtə/ | flapped, like /d/ — /ˈwɑːdər/ |
| **/æ/ vs /ɑː/** | *bath, dance, can't* = /ɑː/ | = /æ/ |
| **/ɒ/** | *hot, not* = /ɒ/ | = /ɑː/ |
| **schedule** | /ˈʃedjuːl/ | /ˈskedʒuːl/ |
| **either** | /ˈaɪðə/ | /ˈiːðər/ |
| **advertisement** | /ədˈvɜːtɪsmənt/ | /ˌædvərˈtaɪzmənt/ |
| **Stress differences** | **BAL**let, **GA**rage, ad**DRESS** | bal**LET**, ga**RAGE**, **AD**dress |

:::key
**Choose one and be consistent.**
Both are fully accepted. Mixing them within a sentence — American /r/ with British vowels — is the only thing that sounds odd. Pick whichever you have had more exposure to and stay with it.
**Listening, however, requires both.** IELTS recordings use British, American, Australian, New Zealand and Canadian speakers. Train your ear on all of them.
:::

## 11.9 A pronunciation training routine

:::key
**Twenty minutes a day**
**5 min — Minimal pairs.** Pick the two contrasts your language struggles with. Say ten pairs aloud. Record yourself; play it back; can *you* hear the difference?
**5 min — Shadowing.** Play 20&ndash;30 seconds of a native speaker. Play it again and speak *simultaneously*, copying rhythm, stress and melody rather than just words. Then record yourself doing it alone and compare.
**5 min — Word stress.** Take ten new words from your vocabulary list. Look up the stress. Say each five times with exaggerated stress.
**5 min — Reading aloud.** Read a paragraph, marking stressed words in advance. Read it with deliberate rhythm and intonation.
:::

**Shadowing is the highest-return exercise in this chapter.** It trains rhythm, intonation, connected speech, stress and fluency simultaneously, and it requires no teacher. Twenty minutes a day for six weeks produces audible change.

## 11.10 Pronunciation self-check

Record two minutes of yourself speaking. Then listen with these questions:

- Can I identify the stressed syllable in every multi-syllable word I used?
- Did my pitch move, or was I flat?
- Did I finish my word endings, or swallow the consonants (*-ed*, *-s*, *-t*)?
- Did I pause at clause boundaries, or in random places?
- Did I speak fast enough to sound natural and slow enough to be clear?
- Are there any words a listener would have to guess at?

The last question is the only one that really matters. **Intelligibility is the criterion.**
