# Chapter 4 — Academic Writing Task 1
^ Twenty minutes, 150 words, one third of the Writing mark: describing data without opinion

The course returns to this task repeatedly, and its treatment is unusually systematic: a fixed four-paragraph architecture, a fixed word count, a fixed time allocation, and a long list of prohibitions. What follows is that system, made complete.

## 4.1 The brief, and what "one third" means

You are given a visual — a graph, chart, table, diagram or map — and asked to *summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant*. You have **twenty minutes** and must write **at least 150 words**.

:::key
**The three numbers that govern this task**
**20 minutes.** Not 25. The task is worth one third of the Writing band; Task 2 is worth two thirds. Time stolen from Task 2 is expensive.
**150 words minimum.** Under 150 and you are penalised automatically on Task Achievement.
**160&ndash;190 words is the target.** The teacher is explicit: "an average of 160 up to 180 words is more than enough." Beyond about 200 you are adding risk — more sentences, more errors, less time — for no reward.
:::

The instruction wording itself contains the entire marking scheme. *Summarise* — do not list everything. *Select* — choose what matters. *Main features* — the big movements, not the small ones. *Make comparisons* — the marks are in the relationships between the data, not in the data.

:::warn
**The prohibitions**
**No opinion.** Never write "I think", "in my view", "this is worrying", "the government should". Task 1 is description. Opinion is off-task and costs Task Achievement directly.
**No explanation of causes** unless the visual shows them. Do not speculate why tourism rose.
**No headings, no subheadings, no bullet points, no numbered lists.** The teacher is emphatic on this: "Never use headings. Never use bullet points. Never use one, two, three, four, five." It must be continuous prose in paragraphs.
**No idioms.** Task 1 is formal academic register.
**No copying the question.** The introduction must be a paraphrase. Copied words are excluded from the word count and damage Lexical Resource.
:::

## 4.2 The four-paragraph architecture

Every Task 1 answer uses the same structure. Internalise it so completely that you never have to think about it in the exam.

| Paragraph | Function | Sentences | Approx. words |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Introduction** | Paraphrase the prompt: what, where, when, units | 1&ndash;2 | 25&ndash;35 |
| **2. Overview** | The 2&ndash;4 biggest features. No numbers. | 2&ndash;3 | 40&ndash;50 |
| **3. Body 1** | First group of detail, with figures | 3&ndash;4 | 45&ndash;55 |
| **4. Body 2** | Second group of detail, with figures | 3&ndash;4 | 45&ndash;55 |

Four paragraphs. Occasionally three if the data is simple, or five if there are two distinct visuals. Never one. Never seven.

:::key
**The overview is the highest-value paragraph in Task 1.**
An answer with no overview is capped at **Band 5** for Task Achievement no matter how accurate the detail. The overview is where you demonstrate that you have *understood* the data rather than merely transcribed it. Write it second, immediately after the introduction, so that you cannot run out of time and omit it.
:::

### What goes in the overview

Two to four statements of the largest, most general patterns. Ask yourself:

1. What is the **overall trend** — is everything rising, falling, or mixed?
2. What is the **highest** and what is the **lowest**?
3. What is the **biggest change** over the period?
4. Is there a **crossover**, a **reversal**, or a **striking similarity**?

And what does *not* go in: specific figures. The overview is the wide shot. Save the numbers for the body paragraphs.

> **Weak overview:** "The graph shows that tourist arrivals in France were 60 million in 1995 and 77 million in 2010, while Egypt rose from 3 million to 14 million."
> **Strong overview:** "Overall, all five countries experienced growth in tourist arrivals over the fifteen-year period, although the scale of this growth varied considerably. France remained by far the most popular destination throughout, whereas the most dramatic proportional increases occurred in Egypt and Malaysia."

The second version has no figures, states three separate patterns, and uses comparison language. That is a Band 7+ overview.

## 4.3 Paragraph 1: the introduction

One sentence, sometimes two. It must paraphrase the question. Four elements must be changed or restated:

| Element | Prompt | Your paraphrase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The visual noun** | "The bar chart below shows..." | "The bar chart provided illustrates..." |
| **The verb** | "shows" | "illustrates / depicts / compares / presents / gives data on" |
| **The topic** | "the number of international tourist arrivals" | "how many overseas visitors travelled to..." |
| **The time frame** | "between 1995 and 2010" | "over a fifteen-year period from 1995 to 2010" |

**Verb bank for the introduction**

| Chart type | Verbs |
| --- | --- |
| Any | illustrates, shows, depicts, presents, provides information on, gives data on, compares, outlines |
| Trends over time | charts, traces, tracks |
| Process | outlines the stages by which, describes the process of |
| Map | illustrates the changes to, shows the development of |

**Chart-type nouns:** line graph, bar chart, column chart, pie chart, table, diagram, flow chart, process diagram, map, plan.

:::warn
**Do not reuse the same verb twice.** If the introduction says "illustrates", the overview cannot also say "illustrates". The teacher makes this point directly: "after that you need to change the verb phrase — don't use the word illustrate one more time."
:::

### Introduction templates

- *The [chart type] [verb] [paraphrased topic] [paraphrased time frame].*
- *The provided [chart type] compares [X] in/between [Y] over the period [Z].*
- *[Paraphrased topic] over [time frame] is illustrated in the [chart type].*

**Worked example.**

> **Prompt:** "The line graph below shows the number of international tourist arrivals in five countries between 1995 and 2010."
> **Introduction:** "The line graph provides data on how many overseas visitors travelled to five different nations over a fifteen-year period beginning in 1995."

Every content word has been changed: *shows* &rarr; *provides data on*; *the number of* &rarr; *how many*; *international tourist arrivals* &rarr; *overseas visitors travelled*; *countries* &rarr; *nations*; *between 1995 and 2010* &rarr; *over a fifteen-year period beginning in 1995*.

:::warn
**Do not paraphrase into inaccuracy.** "Fifteen-year period" is only correct if the period really is fifteen years. If the data runs 1995&ndash;2010 inclusive by decade markers, count carefully. The teacher warns: "you need to be very accurate and true about that."
:::

## 4.4 The language of change: the master bank

This is the vocabulary engine of Task 1. Learn it as **verb + adverb** and **adjective + noun** pairs, because both patterns are needed for grammatical range.

### Upward movement

| Verb | Noun |
| --- | --- |
| rise (rose, risen) | a rise |
| increase | an increase |
| grow (grew, grown) | growth |
| climb | a climb |
| go up | — |
| surge | a surge |
| soar | — |
| rocket | — |
| escalate | an escalation |
| double / triple / quadruple | — |

### Downward movement

| Verb | Noun |
| --- | --- |
| fall (fell, fallen) | a fall |
| decrease | a decrease |
| decline | a decline |
| drop | a drop |
| dip | a dip |
| plunge | a plunge |
| plummet | — |
| slump | a slump |
| tumble | — |
| halve | — |

### Stability, fluctuation, extremes

| Meaning | Language |
| --- | --- |
| No change | remained stable / steady / constant / unchanged; levelled off; plateaued; held steady at |
| Irregular movement | fluctuated; oscillated; varied; showed considerable volatility |
| Peak | reached a peak of; peaked at; hit a high of; reached its highest point |
| Trough | reached a trough of; bottomed out at; hit a low of; reached its lowest point |
| Recovery | recovered; rebounded; bounced back; picked up |

### Degree — adverbs and adjectives

| Degree | Adverb (with verb) | Adjective (with noun) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Very large | dramatically, sharply, steeply, markedly | a dramatic/sharp/steep/marked rise |
| Large | significantly, considerably, substantially | a significant/considerable/substantial increase |
| Moderate | moderately, noticeably | a moderate/noticeable decline |
| Small | slightly, marginally, somewhat | a slight/marginal/minor fall |
| Steady | steadily, gradually, consistently, progressively | a steady/gradual/consistent growth |
| Sudden | suddenly, abruptly, rapidly, swiftly | a sudden/abrupt/rapid drop |

:::key
**The two grammatical patterns — use both**
**Pattern A (verb + adverb):** *Sales rose sharply between 2001 and 2004.*
**Pattern B (there + be + adjective + noun):** *There was a sharp rise in sales between 2001 and 2004.*
**Pattern C (noun as subject):** *A sharp rise in sales occurred between 2001 and 2004.*
Alternating between these three is one of the cheapest ways to raise your Grammatical Range score. Most Band 5 scripts use Pattern A exclusively.
:::

### The synonym-grouping exercise

The course runs an exercise worth reproducing: match expressions that mean the same thing. Understanding these equivalences prevents accidental repetition.

| Group | Equivalent expressions |
| --- | --- |
| Slow steady rise | rose steadily / increased gradually / showed a gradual upward trend / climbed progressively |
| Fast large rise | rose dramatically / surged / soared / increased sharply / rocketed |
| Slow steady fall | fell steadily / declined gradually / edged down |
| Fast large fall | plummeted / plunged / dropped sharply / slumped |
| No change | remained constant / stayed the same / showed no change / levelled off |
| Reached maximum | peaked at / reached a peak of / hit its highest point of |
| Reached minimum | reached a trough of / bottomed out at / fell to a low of |

## 4.5 The grammar of describing data

### Prepositions — the commonest error zone

| Preposition | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **from ... to** | start and end values | "rose *from* 20% *to* 45%" |
| **between ... and** | start and end times | "*between* 1995 *and* 2010" |
| **by** | the size of the change | "increased *by* 25 percentage points" |
| **to** | the final value | "fell *to* just 5 million" |
| **at** | a static value | "stood *at* 40%"; "peaked *at* 62" |
| **in** | year, month, season | "*in* 2004", "*in* March" |
| **on** | specific date | "*on* 3 June" |
| **of** | with a noun of change | "a rise *of* 12%" |
| **over** | duration | "*over* the period", "*over* two decades" |
| **during** | within a period | "*during* the 1990s" |

:::warn
**"Increased by" versus "increased to"**
"Sales increased **by** 20%" = sales grew, and the amount of growth was 20%.
"Sales increased **to** 20%" = sales ended at 20%.
These mean completely different things, and confusing them is a factual error, which damages Task Achievement, not just grammar.
:::

### Tense

**Past data &rarr; past simple.** "In 1995, arrivals stood at 60 million."
**Ongoing/present data &rarr; present simple.** "The chart shows..."
**Future projections &rarr; future forms.** "Figures are expected to reach 90 million by 2030." / "are projected to" / "are forecast to".
**Mixed periods** — switch tenses accordingly, and signal clearly.

### Articles

- **The** with superlatives and specific references: *the highest figure*, *the period in question*, *the graph*.
- **A/an** with a countable noun of change: *a slight increase*, *a dramatic fall*.
- **No article** with plural generalisations: *tourist arrivals rose*, *figures for France*.

### Comparatives and superlatives — the comparison marks

The instruction says "make comparisons where relevant." These are marks you must actively claim.

| Structure | Example |
| --- | --- |
| more/less + adj + than | "France was *more popular than* any other destination." |
| -er than | "Egypt grew *faster than* Malaysia." |
| the most/least + adj | "the *most significant* increase" |
| by far the + superlative | "*by far the* highest figure" |
| twice/three times as many as | "*three times as many* visitors *as* Brazil" |
| nearly/roughly/approximately | "*roughly* twice the figure for Egypt" |
| whereas / while / in contrast | "France rose modestly, *whereas* Egypt more than quadrupled." |
| compared with / in comparison with | "*Compared with* 1995, the 2010 figure was..." |
| a similar pattern / likewise / similarly | "Brazil followed *a similar pattern*." |
| the gap between X and Y widened/narrowed | "*The gap between* the two *widened* after 2003." |

:::key
**Comparison is the difference between Band 6 and Band 7 in Task 1.**
A Band 6 script describes each line separately, one after another. A Band 7 script *relates* the lines to each other: which is higher, which grew faster, where they crossed, how the gap changed. Write at least three explicit comparisons in every answer.
:::

## 4.6 Using figures correctly

The teacher is unambiguous: "You need to add values, you need to add percentage or numbers... you need to support that with figures."

:::warn
**An answer with no figures cannot exceed Band 5** for Task Achievement, because you have not reported the data. But an answer that lists *every* figure is equally penalised, because you have not *selected*. Roughly **six to ten** well-chosen figures across the two body paragraphs is right.
:::

### Which figures to choose

- Start and end values for each major series.
- Peaks and troughs.
- The value at any crossover point.
- The size of the largest change.

### Approximation language — use it

You rarely need exact readings, and pretending to precision you cannot have looks worse than approximating.

| Approximate | Just under | Just over | Rounding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| approximately | just under | just over | around |
| roughly | slightly below | slightly above | about |
| nearly | almost | a little more than | some |
| in the region of | marginally less than | in excess of | or so |

*"...peaked at just over 60 million in 2008 before dipping to around 55 million the following year."*

### Percentages versus proportions

- **per cent** (two words, British) or **percent**; the symbol **%** is acceptable.
- **percentage point** for a difference between percentages: from 20% to 25% is "a rise of five percentage points" or "a rise of 25%". Both are correct but they mean different things — choose deliberately.
- **Proportion / share / fraction:** "one in four", "a quarter", "a third", "two-thirds", "the majority", "a minority", "a significant proportion".

## 4.7 Type-by-type technique

### Line graph — change over time

The default type. Structure the body paragraphs by **grouping**: put similar lines together.

- If two lines rise and two fall: Body 1 = the risers, Body 2 = the fallers.
- If one line dominates: Body 1 = the dominant line, Body 2 = the rest.
- If there are two distinct phases in time: Body 1 = phase one, Body 2 = phase two.

**Model answer — line graph (Band 8).**

> *Prompt: The line graph shows international tourist arrivals (millions) in five countries, 1995&ndash;2010.*

"The line graph provides data on the number of overseas visitors received by five nations over a fifteen-year period from 1995 to 2010.

Overall, every country under consideration saw its visitor numbers grow across the period, though the rate of expansion differed markedly between them. France was the leading destination throughout, while the most striking proportional gains were recorded by Egypt and Malaysia, both of which started from a very low base.

Beginning with the two dominant destinations, France attracted around 60 million tourists in 1995, a figure which climbed steadily to reach approximately 77 million by 2010. The USA followed a broadly similar trajectory, rising from roughly 43 million to just over 60 million, although its growth was interrupted by a noticeable dip between 2000 and 2003, after which numbers recovered.

The remaining three countries operated on a far smaller scale but grew considerably faster in relative terms. Malaysia's arrivals increased almost fivefold, from under 8 million to approximately 25 million, overtaking Brazil in 2001 and continuing to pull away thereafter. Egypt's figures more than quadrupled, climbing from some 3 million to around 14 million, whereas Brazil recorded the most modest expansion of the five, edging up from roughly 2 million to just 5 million over the entire period."

*Word count: 196. Four paragraphs. Overview with no figures. Eleven figures, all approximated. Six explicit comparisons. Three grammatical patterns for change.*

### Bar chart — comparison between categories

If there is no time axis, you are comparing categories, so comparison language dominates and trend verbs largely disappear. Group by **size** (highest group, lowest group) or by **category type**.

Useful frames: *X accounted for the largest share, at...* / *At the other end of the scale...* / *The three remaining categories were broadly comparable, ranging from ... to ...*

### Pie chart — proportions of a whole

Almost always about **share**. Key vocabulary: *accounted for*, *made up*, *constituted*, *represented*, *comprised*.

- "Housing accounted for the largest share of expenditure, at 32%."
- "Transport and food together made up nearly half of the total."
- "Entertainment represented the smallest proportion, at a mere 4%."

With **two pie charts** (two years or two groups), the task is comparison, not description. Do not describe pie 1 then pie 2. Describe what **changed**: which shares grew, which shrank, which stayed the same, whether the ranking changed.

### Table — numbers in a grid

Tables often contain more data than you can report, so selection is being tested hardest here. Find the extremes: highest and lowest in each row and column, and any cell that breaks the pattern. Organise your body paragraphs by row or by column, whichever gives the cleaner story.

### Process diagram — no time axis, no figures

A radically different task with its own language.

:::key
**Process diagrams: the five rules**
1. **Count the stages** and state the number in the overview: "The process comprises seven distinct stages, beginning with X and culminating in Y."
2. **State whether it is linear or cyclical.**
3. **Use the passive voice** throughout: "the beans *are harvested*", "the mixture *is then heated* to 180°C". The agent is irrelevant; the process is what matters.
4. **Use sequencers**, not comparison language: *initially, first of all, subsequently, following this, once X has been completed, at this point, in the final stage.*
5. **No figures, no trends, no comparisons.** These verbs do not belong here.
:::

**Sequencing bank:** First / Initially / To begin with / At the first stage &rarr; Next / Then / Subsequently / Following this / After that / Once this has been done / At this point &rarr; Meanwhile / Simultaneously / At the same time &rarr; Finally / Lastly / In the final stage / The process culminates in / The cycle then repeats.

**Passive forms you will need:** is/are + past participle (*is collected*), is/are then + pp (*is then filtered*), having been + pp (*having been dried, the leaves are...*), before/after being + pp (*before being packaged*).

### Maps — change over time in a place

Two maps of the same location at different dates. The task is **change**.

**Language of development:**

| Change | Language |
| --- | --- |
| New construction | was built / was constructed / was erected; a new X was added |
| Removal | was demolished / was knocked down / was cleared / was removed |
| Conversion | was converted into / was turned into / was redeveloped as |
| Expansion | was extended / was enlarged / was expanded |
| Reduction | was reduced in size / was scaled back |
| Replacement | was replaced by / gave way to / made way for |
| Relocation | was relocated to / was moved to |
| Retention | remained unchanged / was left intact / was retained |

Structure by **area** (north / south / centre) or by **type of change** (what was added / what was removed). Use compass directions and spatial prepositions constantly: *in the north-east corner*, *along the western edge*, *on the opposite bank*, *adjacent to*, *to the south of*.

Tense: **past simple passive** for a past-to-past comparison; **present perfect** if one map is "today"; **future passive** ("will be built") if one map shows a plan.

### Multiple visuals

Occasionally you get two charts, or a chart and a table. Do not treat them as two separate tasks. Write one introduction covering both, one overview covering both (stating the relationship between them if there is one), then one body paragraph for each visual.

## 4.8 The Task 1 checklist

Run this in the last two minutes.

- Four paragraphs, clearly separated.
- Introduction paraphrases the prompt — no copied phrases.
- Overview present, labelled with "Overall," or "In general," and containing **no figures**.
- Body paragraphs contain figures, correctly read.
- At least three explicit comparisons.
- No opinion, no explanation of causes, no bullet points, no headings.
- Word count between 160 and 190.
- Tenses consistent with the time frame.
- "by" versus "to" used correctly in every change statement.
- Verbs not repeated more than twice.

## 4.9 Practice tasks

**Task 1A.** *The bar chart shows the percentage of households with internet access in four countries in 2000, 2010 and 2020. Summarise the information. Write at least 150 words. 20 minutes.*

**Task 1B.** *The two pie charts show the proportion of energy generated from six sources in one country in 1990 and 2020.*

**Task 1C.** *The diagram illustrates the process by which recycled paper is produced.*

**Task 1D.** *The two maps show the town of Northfield in 1980 and today.*

**Task 1E.** *The table gives figures for average monthly rainfall and temperature in five cities.*

For each: write in twenty minutes, then self-assess against the checklist in 4.8 before rewriting. The rewrite is where the learning happens.
