# Chapter 12 — Study Plans and Exam-Day Management
^ Turning the preceding eleven chapters into a schedule you will actually follow

Knowledge of technique is worthless without practice hours. This chapter converts the book into three concrete programmes, and then deals with the last twenty-four hours.

## 12.1 Before you choose a plan

Answer three questions:

1. **What is my current band?** From the diagnostic in Chapter 1.5, not from hope.
2. **What band do I need?** The exact requirement, including any per-component minimum. A university asking for "6.5 overall with no band below 6.0" is a different target from "6.5 overall".
3. **How many hours a week can I genuinely give?** Be honest. A plan you abandon in week two is worse than a modest plan you complete.

:::key
**A realistic rate of improvement**
Moving up **half a band** typically requires 80&ndash;120 hours of focused work.
Moving up **a full band** typically requires 200&ndash;300 hours.
This assumes *focused* work: timed practice with error analysis, not passive exposure. Watching English television for 200 hours will not move your band.
If your target requires more than a full band of improvement, book the test for later rather than taking it twice.
:::

## 12.2 The four-week intensive

For candidates already within half a band of their target. Assumes **2&ndash;3 hours a day**.

### Week 1 — Diagnose and rebuild technique

| Day | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Full diagnostic test (Chapter 1.5). Categorise every error. |
| 2 | Chapter 2 (Listening). Two practice sections, with prediction routine. |
| 3 | Chapter 3 (Reading). Skimming drill on five passages; one timed passage. |
| 4 | Chapter 4 or 5 (Task 1). Two tasks in 20 minutes each. |
| 5 | Chapter 6 (Task 2). One essay, then rewrite it with the checklist. |
| 6 | Chapter 7 (Speaking). Record Part 1 &times; 8 questions and one Part 2. Review. |
| 7 | Review the week's errors. Build your error log (Chapter 8.7). |

### Week 2 — Drill the weakest component

Spend **five of seven days** on your lowest component. Add: daily 20-minute pronunciation routine (Chapter 11.9); daily 10 vocabulary chunks (Chapter 10.7).

### Week 3 — Timed practice under pressure

One full Listening and one full Reading every day, timed. One Task 1 and one Task 2 every other day. One recorded full Speaking test every other day.

### Week 4 — Full mocks and consolidation

| Day | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| 22 | Full mock test (L + R + W back to back, 2h40) |
| 23 | Review mock in detail |
| 24 | Weakest component only |
| 25 | Full mock test |
| 26 | Review; rewrite both essays |
| 27 | Light revision: checklists, linking words, topic vocabulary |
| 28 | Rest. No English. |

## 12.3 The eight-week programme

For candidates a full band below target. **1.5&ndash;2 hours a day**.

| Weeks | Focus | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1&ndash;2** | Listening + Reading technique. Chapters 2 and 3 in full, every exercise. Untimed accuracy first. | All ten Listening question types and all nine Reading types understood and practised. |
| **3&ndash;4** | Writing. Chapters 4/5 and 6. Eight Task 1s and eight Task 2s, each written then rewritten. | The four-paragraph and PEEL structures automatic. |
| **5&ndash;6** | Speaking + pronunciation. Chapters 7 and 11. Daily recording. Thirty cue cards. | Two-minute long turn comfortable; Part 1 extension automatic. |
| **7** | Grammar and vocabulary consolidation. Chapters 9 and 10. The ten structures in 9.12 drilled to automaticity. | Error log reduced to fewer than five recurring patterns. |
| **8** | Full mocks: three complete tests, reviewed in detail. | Timing under control; no unattempted questions. |

Throughout all eight weeks, daily: 20 minutes pronunciation, 15 vocabulary chunks per week, 30 minutes reading English non-fiction.

## 12.4 The twelve-week foundation programme

For candidates more than a full band below target, or building from a lower level.

| Weeks | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| **1&ndash;3** | **Language foundation.** Chapter 9 (grammar) systematically: sentence types, tenses, articles, agreement. Chapter 10: 200 collocations. No timed practice yet. |
| **4&ndash;6** | **Receptive skills.** Chapters 2 and 3, untimed then timed. Daily dictation. Daily reading. |
| **7&ndash;9** | **Productive skills.** Chapters 4/5, 6, 7, 11. Twelve essays, twelve Task 1s, daily speaking recordings. |
| **10&ndash;11** | **Integration.** Full timed components. Weekly full mock. Detailed error analysis. |
| **12** | **Taper.** Two mocks early in the week, then light revision only. Rest before the test. |

## 12.5 What effective practice looks like

:::warn
**Three ways candidates waste practice hours**
**1. Doing tests without reviewing them.** A test you have marked but not analysed teaches almost nothing. Budget *as much time for review as for the test itself*.
**2. Practising untimed and hoping timing will look after itself.** It will not. Timing is a separate skill.
**3. Writing essays no one reads, including you.** Write, then wait 24 hours, then mark your own work against the descriptors in Chapter 8, then **rewrite**. The rewrite is where improvement happens.
:::

### The review protocol

For every practice test:

1. Mark it. Record the raw score.
2. For each error, write **why** — not "careless" but the specific cause: *word limit*, *distractor*, *didn't know the synonym*, *ran out of time*, *answered from knowledge*.
3. Tally the causes. After five tests you will see two or three dominating.
4. Design a drill for the dominant cause and do it for a week.
5. Re-test.

## 12.6 The final week

| Days before | Do | Don't |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **7&ndash;5** | Two full mocks. Detailed review. | Don't learn new vocabulary. |
| **4&ndash;3** | Revise checklists, structures, linking words, topic vocabulary. One Task 2 per day. | Don't take more full mocks — the fatigue costs more than the practice gains. |
| **2** | Light revision only. Read your error log. Re-read the four component checklists. | Don't study a new question type. |
| **1** | Rest. Check documents, location, travel time. Sleep. | Don't study at all. Seriously. |

### Practical preparation

- **Documents:** the passport or ID you registered with. No other form is accepted. Check the name matches exactly.
- **Location and time:** know the route; plan to arrive 45 minutes early.
- **Allowed on the desk:** pencils, erasers, a transparent water bottle with the label removed.
- **Not allowed:** phones, watches (in many centres), notes, food. Lockers are provided.
- **Clothing:** layers. Test rooms are frequently too cold or too hot.

## 12.7 On the day

### Listening (30 min + transfer)

- Use every second of the preparation pauses: word limit, keywords, word class, predicted paraphrase.
- Write answers on the question paper as you listen; transfer at the end (paper test).
- Miss one, move on within two seconds.
- Never leave a blank.

### Reading (60 min)

- Twenty minutes per passage. Hard stop.
- Skim first, always.
- Transfer answers passage by passage.
- Never leave a blank.

### Writing (60 min)

- **Task 2 first** if you are prone to overrunning on Task 1 — it carries twice the marks and must be protected. (If you are disciplined with time, the standard order is fine.)
- Task 1: 20 minutes, 160&ndash;190 words, overview mandatory.
- Task 2: 40 minutes — 5 plan, 30 write, 5 check.
- Check for: articles, plurals, agreement, tense, word count, paragraphing.

### Speaking (11&ndash;14 min)

- Arrive with your mouth warmed up. Speak English aloud for ten minutes before you go in — to a friend, to yourself, to a wall.
- Part 1: two to three sentences, 20&ndash;40 seconds, always a reason.
- Part 2: use the full minute to write keywords; speak for the full two minutes.
- Part 3: Answer &rarr; Reason &rarr; Example &rarr; Balance. Hedge. Ask for clarification in English if you need it.
- If you make a mistake, correct it once briefly and continue. Do not dwell; do not apologise.

## 12.8 Managing anxiety

Three evidence-based points, all consistent with the advice the course gives its nervous students.

**1. Familiarity displaces fear.** The format itself should be boring by test day. If your fortieth mock Part 2 still frightens you, do ten more.

**2. Physical control precedes mental control.** Slow exhalation lengthens; drop the shoulders; unclench the jaw. Two minutes of slow breathing before the Speaking test measurably reduces the physiological symptoms that interfere with articulation.

**3. Reframe the examiner.** They are not judging you as a person. They are completing a form against published criteria. Their neutrality is professional, not personal.

:::key
**And the single most useful thought to hold on the day:**
You are not being asked to be a native speaker. You are being asked to demonstrate, within a known format, the specific behaviours listed in a published rubric. Every one of those behaviours is in this book, and every one of them is trainable.
:::
