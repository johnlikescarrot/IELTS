## 7.2 True / False / Not Given (Identifying Information)

**The most-feared type — and the most mechanical once you learn the rules.**

Definitions (memorise):

- **TRUE:** the statement matches the passage's information (same meaning, different words allowed).
- **FALSE:** the statement contradicts the passage (one contradicting detail is enough).
- **NOT GIVEN:** the information is absent — the passage neither confirms nor denies it.

**Method:**

1. Read the statement; underline the **claim** (the one testable proposition) + 2 keywords.
2. Locate via keywords (in order — bracket between neighbours).
3. Compare claim vs passage text word by word: same? opposite? unmentioned?
4. Default rule: if you cannot find it after 90 seconds, it is probably NG — but only after a genuine search.

**The five contradiction patterns (FALSE triggers):**

1. **Opposite:** *cheap ↔ expensive; increased ↔ decreased.*
2. **Different number/date/quantity:** *1843 ↔ 1845; two million ↔ three million.*
3. **Different person/place/purpose:** *profit from cargo ↔ profit as a tourist attraction.*
4. **Changed strength:** *believed to be ↔ was; some ↔ all; often ↔ always; can ↔ must.*
5. **Changed time:** *was used by pedestrians ↔ is used by pedestrians (now part of the Underground).*

**The three NG patterns:**

1. **New detail:** statues/souvenirs "sold" (passage: tunnel *became* an attraction *selling souvenirs* — buying statues specifically? unmentioned → NG).
2. **Comparison without basis:** "more crowded than..." when the passage gives no comparison.
3. **Opinion about fact:** passage gives facts; statement adds an evaluation nobody stated.

>>> FULL WORKED EXAMPLE — The Thames Tunnel (Video Parts 1/4–5, Exercise 1). Passage (abridged to the tested sentences): "The Thames Tunnel was a tunnel built under the River Thames in London. It was the first subaqueous tunnel ever built and many believed it was the Eighth Wonder of the World at the time it was opened. It was opened in 1843 to pedestrians only and people came from far and wide to see the marvel... The working class used it for its functional use of crossing from one side of the river to another, using boats and ships in the sea... In the age of sail and horse-drawn coaches, people travelled a long way to visit the tunnel... The aim had been for the tunnel to be used by wheeled vehicles to transport cargo so that it could bring in a profit. But this failed and the tunnel eventually became nothing more than a tourist attraction selling souvenirs. In 1865, the tunnel became part of the London Underground railway system which continues to be its use today."
>>> Q1 "The Thames Tunnel was the first tunnel ever built under a river." → first = first; under a river = subaqueous. Match → TRUE.
>>> Q2 "The Thames Tunnel was the Eighth Wonder of the World." → Passage: many BELIEVED it was. Belief ≠ fact; claim strengthened → FALSE.
>>> Q3 "People were drawn from all over to see the Thames Tunnel." → drawn from all over = came from far and wide; to see = to see the marvel. Match → TRUE.
>>> Q4 "People were able to travel by sea or land in those days." → boats/ships (sea) + sail/horse-drawn coaches (land). Match → TRUE.
>>> Q5 "The aim of the tunnel was to turn a profit as a tourist attraction." → Passage: aim = profit via CARGO; tourist attraction = what it became after failure. Purpose swapped → FALSE.
>>> Q6 "Statues of the tunnel could be bought as souvenirs." → Souvenirs mentioned; statues specifically? Absent → NOT GIVEN.
>>> Q7 "The tunnel is no longer used as a pedestrian walkway to cross the river." → Now part of the Underground; pedestrian crossing ended. Match → TRUE.

**Drills:**

1. Classify 20 statements from any TFNG set by contradiction pattern (1–5) or NG pattern (1–3) before checking answers.
2. Rewrite 10 TRUE statements as FALSE (change one detail) and 10 as NG (add an unmentioned detail) — then swap with a partner.
3. Timed TFNG: 7 questions in 8 minutes, target 6+.

## 7.3 Yes / No / Not Given (Writer's Views)

Identical technique — but you judge the **writer's claims/opinions**, usually in discursive Passage 3 texts:

- **YES:** the statement matches the writer's view.
- **NO:** it contradicts the writer's view.
- **NOT GIVEN:** the writer expresses no view on it.

Signal phrases for opinions: *I believe, it is clear that, undoubtedly, should, must, surprisingly, unfortunately, critics argue, proponents claim.* Facts without evaluation cannot support Y/N — they push toward NG.

> Trap: a statement can be factually true in real life but NG if the *writer* never claims it. Judge the text, not the world.

## 7.4 Matching Headings

**Looks like:** 5–7 paragraphs (A–G) + 8–10 headings (i–x, one or two spares). Instruction: *Choose the correct heading for each paragraph.*

**Method:**

1. Read all headings first; simplify each to 2–3 words (*ii = spice costs; v = early beliefs*).
2. Do headings LAST within the passage (you'll know the paragraphs already).
3. For each paragraph: first sentence + last sentence + skim middle → match the **whole-paragraph main idea**.
4. Eliminate used headings; spares are usually too narrow (one detail) or too broad (whole-text theme).

**The #1 trap:** a heading that matches one vivid example sentence but not the paragraph. If a heading fits only one sentence, it is wrong — headings summarise *paragraphs*.

**Drills:**

1. Heading simplification: reduce 10 headings to 3 words each in 3 minutes.
2. First/last drill: match 5 paragraphs using only first + last sentences, then verify by full read.
3. Spare-spotting: for 3 sets, explain in writing why each spare heading is wrong.

## 7.5 Matching Information / Features / Sentence Endings

**Information to paragraph (A–G):** *Which paragraph contains...?* — detail hunt. Keywords are specific (numbers, names, technical terms). Skim for the detail, then verify. Answers are NOT in order; one paragraph may hold two answers.

**Features (names/dates/theories to descriptions):** Match each feature (researcher, company, period) to its statement. Method: scan for the feature name (easy to find), read around it, match the claim. Names appear in order of the *features list*, not the passage.

**Sentence endings (A–H):** 3–6 sentence beginnings + a bank of endings. Grammar is your filter: match the beginning's frame (*...caused by... → noun phrase; ...because... → clause*), then verify meaning in the passage. In-order — the fastest matching type.

> For all matching: **eliminate as you go** (cross out used options), and never re-read the whole passage per question — one careful read should serve the entire set.

## 7.6 Sentence & Summary Completion

**Looks like:** gapped sentences (*Complete the sentences. Choose NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage for each answer.*) or a summary paragraph with gaps — with or without a word bank.

**Method (no bank):**

1. Read the sentence; predict the word class and meaning of the gap.
2. Locate via keywords (in order).
3. Copy the exact word(s) from the passage — spelling, number, and form unchanged.
4. Re-read the completed sentence: grammar must fit.

**Method (with bank A–H):** Grammar-filter first (which options fit the frame?), then meaning-match in the passage. Bank options are designed so 2–3 fit grammatically — the passage decides.

>>> The spices passage (Video Part 3, Exercise 2) is classic summary-completion territory: "In the early days, spices were thought to have strong medicinal properties... thought to prevent maladies such as the plague... spices flavoured the bland meat-based European cuisines... Pepper... has always been in highest demand... saffron... the world's most costly spice by weight, valued mostly for its vivid colour." A summary gap like "Early Europeans valued spices as ... and as protection against ..." resolves to "medicines" (medicinal properties → paraphrase) and "disease/the plague" — note how the question paraphrases (valued = thought/demand; protection = prevent) while the answer words come from the text.

**Drills:**

1. Grammar-filter: for 10 banked gaps, list fitting options by grammar before reading the passage.
2. Exact-copy: complete 10 gaps, then verify letter-by-letter against the passage.
3. Paraphrase pairs: log every question↔passage synonym from 2 summary sets.

## 7.7 Notes / Table / Flow-Chart / Diagram Completion

Same locating + copying skills in structured layouts:

- **Notes/table:** use filled cells as anchors (as in Listening tables); answers in row order.
- **Flow-chart:** follows a process in sequence — perfect for process passages; each stage has one gap.
- **Diagram labelling:** parts of a machine/structure/process. Scan for the part name near the gap number, read the description, match.

> Diagram labels are the easiest marks in Reading: the diagram itself shows you where to look. Never leave them blank.

## 7.8 Multiple Choice (Single & Multiple)

Same distractor logic as Listening (Chapter 4.5), but the "recording" is text you can re-read:

1. Read the stem; locate its paragraph (keywords, in order).
2. Read the located zone at detail speed BEFORE reading options (form your own answer).
3. Match your answer to the options' paraphrases; eliminate the rest with evidence.
4. For "choose TWO": verify each option independently — one is often half-right.

! Options use the passage's vocabulary against you: the wrong options recycle passage words with changed meaning; the right option paraphrases. Trust meaning, never word-matching.

## 7.9 Short Answer

*Answer the questions. Choose NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS from the passage.* — Scanning questions: who/what/when/where/how many. Locate (names/numbers scan fast), copy exactly, mind the limit. Usually the quickest set — do them first within a passage.

## 7.10 FULL WORKED SET — Yes / No / Not Given (Writer's Views)

**Mini-passage (opinion text):** "Urban green spaces are often praised as the 'lungs of the city', yet I find this celebration naive. While parks undoubtedly improve air quality, city councils routinely exaggerate their social benefits. Claims that a new park 'brings communities together' rarely survive scrutiny: in my own borough, the £2 million Riverside Park is used overwhelmingly by affluent newcomers, while long-term residents report feeling excluded. This is not an argument against parks — they remain essential — but against the lazy assumption that planting trees automatically heals divided neighbourhoods. What actually builds community is programming: free events, sports leagues, and genuinely mixed housing nearby. Without these, a park is just attractive scenery for those who can already afford it."

**Q1 The writer believes parks have no real environmental value.** → Passage: "parks undoubtedly improve air quality" — contradicts "no value" → NO.
**Q2 The writer claims councils overstate the social effects of parks.** → "routinely exaggerate their social benefits" — direct match → YES.
**Q3 Riverside Park cost more than most similar projects.** → £2 million given; comparison with others? Absent → NOT GIVEN.
**Q4 The writer opposes building new parks.** → "not an argument against parks — they remain essential" → NO.
**Q5 The writer considers organised activities more important than the park itself for community life.** → "What actually builds community is programming" → YES.
**Q6 Local residents participated in designing Riverside Park.** → Design process unmentioned → NOT GIVEN.

*Judge the writer's claims, not the facts: Q3's £2m is factual but the comparison is the writer's silence → NG.*

## 7.11 FULL WORKED SET — Matching Headings

**Passage (4 paragraphs, 5 headings):**

**A.** Honeybees recognise human faces. In 2019, French researchers trained bees to associate photographs of faces with sugar rewards; the bees subsequently picked the familiar faces from line-ups with 80% accuracy — despite brains smaller than a pinhead. The finding forces a rethink of how much neural machinery recognition really requires.

**B.** The mechanism, surprisingly, resembles our own: bees assemble faces from configurations of features rather than memorising whole images. When researchers scrambled the features, recognition collapsed — exactly as with humans viewing jumbled faces. Simple circuits, it seems, can run sophisticated algorithms.

**C.** Engineers are already copying the trick. Drones that navigate using bee-style vision need a fraction of the computing power of conventional systems, promising longer flight times and cheaper hardware. Several start-ups now market "bio-inspired" navigation chips explicitly modelled on insect brains.

**D.** There are limits. Bees recognise only a handful of faces after lengthy training; no bee will ever pick a suspect from a parade. But the deeper lesson stands: intelligence is not about brain size but about efficient wiring — a humbling thought for a species proud of its three-pound brain.

**Headings:** i. Commercial applications of the discovery / ii. How bees process faces / iii. An astonishing experimental result / iv. The boundaries of bee intelligence / v. Why humans recognise faces easily.

**Answers:** A–iii (the 80% result = astonishing; "forces a rethink"), B–ii (mechanism: configurations of features), C–i (drones, chips, start-ups = commercial applications), D–iv (limits: handful of faces; lesson). Spare: v (humans' ease never discussed — classic too-broad spare).

*Method check: headings done after reading the whole passage; each heading matches the paragraph's main thrust, not one example; spare v is topically related but textually unsupported.*

## 7.12 FULL WORKED SET — Matching Features

**Passage:** "Three architects shaped the modern library. **Andrew Carnegie** (1835–1919) funded over 2,500 library buildings worldwide, insisting each town contribute the site and maintenance — philanthropy with conditions. **Henri Labrouste** (1801–1875) pioneered light-filled reading rooms with exposed iron frames, most famously in Paris, proving libraries could be both functional and sublime. **Rem Koolhaas** (1944–) reimagined libraries as social warehouses: his Seattle Central Library (2004) stacks eleven floors of mixed uses — books, cafés, auditoriums — around a spiral of continuous shelving."

**Match each architect (1–3) to a statement (A–E):** A required local co-funding / B designed a spiral shelving system / C worked mainly in Paris / D opposed decorative architecture / E mixed books with non-book facilities.

**Answers:** 1 Carnegie–A (site + maintenance conditions), 2 Labrouste–C (Paris; D is the trap — "functional AND sublime" contradicts "opposed decorative"), 3 Koolhaas–B and E (both true of Seattle — in real tests each feature matches once; here B is the unique match and E is its paraphrase — exam sets avoid this; train by choosing the BEST match: B).

## 7.13 FULL WORKED SET — Summary Completion + Diagram Label

**Passage (condensed):** "The Passivhaus standard originated in Germany in the early 1990s. Buildings are wrapped in super-insulation up to 40 cm thick, fitted with triple-glazed windows, and sealed almost airtight. A mechanical ventilation unit then recovers heat from outgoing stale air and transfers it to incoming fresh air, cutting heating demand by roughly 90% compared with conventional buildings. The main obstacle is cost: certification adds 5–8% to construction budgets, though advocates note the premium is typically recovered within a decade through energy savings."

**Summary (no bank):** "Passivhaus buildings use thick 1 ... and triple glazing, and are sealed to be nearly 2 .... A ventilation system recycles heat from 3 ... air. Although building costs rise by 4 ..., the extra expense is usually repaid within 5 ...."

**Answers:** 1 (super-)insulation, 2 airtight, 3 stale (accept: outgoing stale), 4 5–8% (accept: five to eight percent), 5 a decade (accept: ten years? NO — must copy passage words: "a decade").

*Lesson: Q5's trap — "ten years" is correct in meaning but the instruction says words FROM THE PASSAGE → "a decade". Meaning gets you to the location; copying rules get you the mark.*

**Diagram label drill (same passage):** a cross-section labels A (wall layer), B (window), C (ventilation box). Method: match each label's description sentence (*wrapped in super-insulation → A; triple-glazed → B; recovers heat → C*). Diagrams test whether you can map text to image — always the fastest marks in the set.

## 7.14 Question-Order Strategy Per Passage

Optimal order (write it on your question paper):

1. Short answer + completion (fast, in-order, build passage knowledge).
2. TFNG / YNNQ (need detail; passage now familiar).
3. Multiple choice + sentence endings (targeted re-reads).
4. Matching information/features (need whole-passage sense).
5. Headings (last — one skim per paragraph suffices now).

> Video: the course demonstrates this "easy-first" principle when it teaches completion before opinions, and facts before headings. Your goal is 40 marks — collect the cheap ones first.
