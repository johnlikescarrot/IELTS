# Chapter 11: Writing — How You Are Scored & How to Build Sentences

## 11.1 The Two Tasks and Their Weight

You write two tasks in 60 minutes. Task 2 counts **twice as much** as Task 1 — budget your time accordingly:

| Task | Words (minimum) | Target length | Time | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Task 1: report (Academic) or letter (GT) | 150+ | 160–190 | 20 minutes (3 plan + 15 write + 2 check) | 1/3 |
| Task 2: essay (both modules) | 250+ | 260–290 | 40 minutes (5 plan + 32 write + 3 check) | 2/3 |

> Video Parts 8–12 teach Writing through the scoring criteria first: the course opens the module with IDP's *How you'll be scored* wheel (four criteria × 25%) for Task 1 (Task Achievement) and Task 2 (Task Response). Internalise the criteria before memorising any template — examiners score criteria, not templates.

## 11.2 The Four Criteria (25% Each)

### Task Achievement (T1) / Task Response (T2)

- **T1:** cover all key features with an overview; no irrelevant detail; no opinion. Under-length (<150 words) caps this criterion at Band 5.
- **T2:** answer every part of the question with a clear position sustained throughout; develop each main idea; under-length (<250 words) caps at Band 5.
- Missing the question (e.g., describing only one of two charts; agreeing without addressing "to what extent") caps T2 at Band 5–6 whatever your English level.

### Coherence and Cohesion (CC)

- **Coherence:** logical organisation — Task 1: introduction → overview → details (grouped); Task 2: introduction → body 1 → body 2 (+3) → conclusion. One clear central idea per paragraph.
- **Cohesion:** linking within and between sentences: reference (*this, these, such, it*), substitution, ellipsis, and cohesive devices (linkers). Band 7: "logically organises information; clear progression; range of cohesive devices with occasional misuse." Band 5–6: mechanical overuse (*Firstly... Secondly... Thirdly... In conclusion* in every essay) or faulty links.
- Paragraphing is scored: 4–5 paragraphs for Task 2; 3–4 for Task 1. One-block essays cannot pass Band 5 for CC.

### Lexical Resource (LR)

- **Range:** topic vocabulary, collocations, less common items (*a dramatic surge, to hold the view, stringent regulations*), and precise word choice.
- **Accuracy:** spelling + word formation (*economy/economic/economical/economist*). Occasional slips are fine at Band 7; systematic errors cap Band 6.
- **The course's LR rules (Part 11):** never copy question words — use synonyms (*famous = prominent = renowned; obstacles = hurdles*); prefer less common vocabulary where natural; avoid spelling mistakes by using only words you can spell.
- Memorised phrases (*"a double-edged sword", "every coin has two sides"*) impress no one and can be flagged as memorised — use natural academic collocations instead.

### Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA)

- **Range:** the course's formula (Part 11): simple + compound + complex sentences, deliberately mixed. Complex = subordination (although, because, which, where, if, when, while).
- **Accuracy:** Band 7 = "frequent error-free sentences"; Band 6 = errors rarely impede; Band 5 = errors may impede.
- Punctuation is grammar: commas in complex sentences, full stops (no comma splices), apostrophes in GT informal letters only (never in Academic/contractions in essays — see 11.4).

## 11.3 Sentence Variety — The Course's Grammar Engine (Part 11)

### The three sentence types (with the course's own examples, corrected and expanded)

**1. Simple sentence = Subject + Verb (+ Object/Complement).**
*Dania studied hard. Dania got a high band score.* — Clear but basic; use for impact and clarity, never for whole essays.

**2. Compound sentence = simple + comma + FANBOYS linker (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) + simple.**
*Dania studied hard, so she got a high band score.* / *The internet is useful, but it has some drawbacks.* — Shows coordination; one or two per paragraph maximum.

**3. Complex sentence = subordinate clause + main clause (either order).**
*If you study hard, you will succeed.* / *Although John studied hard, he did not get a good score.* / *In spite of studying hard, John did not get a good score.* — The engine of Band 7+: subordinate with *although, because, since, as, while, whereas, which, who, where, if, when, after, before.*

**Punctuation rules that examiners check:**

| Pattern | Example | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Linker mid-sentence (FANBOYS) | *..., so she... / ..., but it...* | Comma before the linker |
| Subordinate clause first | *Although John studied hard, he...* | Comma after the clause |
| Main clause first | *He failed although he studied hard.* | No comma (usually) |
| Sentence linker (However, Therefore) | *John studied hard. However, he...* | Full stop + capital + comma |
| Semicolon upgrade | *John studied hard; however, he...* | Semicolon before However/Therefore (formal, Band 7+ signal) |
| Despite / In spite of | *Despite studying hard, John... / ...in spite of hard work.* | + noun or + verb-ing (NEVER + clause: not "despite he studied") |

### The concession family (Part 11's core lesson — master all six frames)

1. *Although / Though / Even though + clause:* "Although automation boosts output, it threatens routine jobs."
2. *However + clause (new sentence):* "...routine jobs. However, new roles emerge."
3. *Despite / In spite of + noun/-ing:* "Despite higher output, employment fell."
4. *While / Whereas + clause (contrast):* "While output rose, wages stagnated."
5. *Admittedly..., but...:* "Admittedly, cameras deter crime, but they erode privacy."
6. *Noun-phrase concession:* "Heavy investment notwithstanding, rural coverage remains poor." (Band 8+ flavour)

### The reason/result family

- Reason: *because, since, as, owing to + noun, due to + noun, out of + noun, in order to + verb.*
- Result: *so, therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, which means that..., leading to + noun.*
- Purpose: *to, in order to, so as to, so that + clause, with the aim of + -ing.*

>>> Upgrade drill (do 5 daily): Simple pair → one complex sentence. "Automation reduces manual labour. Working hours should be reduced." → "Since automation has reduced the need for manual labour, working hours should arguably be reduced." / "Because automation has reduced..., governments should consider..." / "Automation having reduced..., ..." (participle — Band 8).

## 11.4 Formal Academic Style (Task 2 + Academic T1)

1. **No contractions:** write *do not, cannot, it is* (contractions cap LR/GRA formality judgments).
2. **No spoken fillers:** never *well, you know, kind of, lots of, a lot of* (use *many, much, a number of, a great deal of*), *thing* (name it), *get* (use *obtain, receive, become* precisely).
3. **No absolutes without hedging:** replace *always/never/proves* with *tends to, is likely to, suggests, indicates, may, can.* Academic writing hedges; only hedge-free claims need 100% proof.
4. **Impersonal stance:** prefer *It is argued that... / This essay will... / Such policies...* over *I think* (one *In my opinion* in intro + conclusion is enough; avoid *In my humble opinion* and *myself*).
5. **Nominalisation (Band 7+ signal):** verbs → nouns: *pollute → pollution; develop → development; "Because cities grow fast, housing fails" → "Rapid urban growth outpaces housing supply."*
6. **Articles and number:** *the internet, the environment, the government* (specific systems); *people* (plural, no article) vs *the people of...*; *research* (uncountable — never "researches").

! GT letters follow different rules: informal letters to friends SHOULD use contractions and friendly tone; formal letters to managers must not. Chapter 12 gives the three tones with models.

## 11.5 Cohesion Toolbelt (60 devices, grouped by function)

**Adding:** *also, in addition, additionally, furthermore, moreover, as well as, not only... but also...*
**Contrasting:** *but, however, nevertheless, nonetheless, although, though, even though, despite, in spite of, whereas, while, on the one hand... on the other hand..., in contrast, conversely, on the contrary.*
**Cause/reason:** *because, since, as, owing to, due to, because of, on account of, given that...*
**Result:** *so, therefore, thus, hence, consequently, as a result, accordingly, for this reason...*
**Sequencing:** *firstly, secondly, finally, subsequently, previously, beforehand, afterwards, meanwhile...*
**Exemplifying:** *for example, for instance, such as, including, namely, to illustrate, a case in point is...*
**Restating:** *in other words, that is to say, i.e., namely...*
**Concluding:** *in conclusion, to conclude, overall, all in all, on balance, taking everything into account...*
**Conceding:** *admittedly, granted, while it is true that..., despite...*
**Referencing (the invisible cohesion):** *this/that/these/those + noun* ("this trend", "such policies"), *the former/the latter, respectively, the above.*

> Band 7 uses these *appropriately*; Band 5–6 overuses a few mechanically. Rule: never the same sentence-linker twice in one paragraph; vary reference + linker + lexical chains (repeat key nouns with synonyms) across the essay.

## 11.6 The Writing Process (every task, every time)

| Stage | Task 1 (20 min) | Task 2 (40 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Analyse (T2: 2 min) | Identify visual type, units, time frame, categories (1 min) | Underline topic words + instruction words; decide position (2 min) |
| Plan | Select 2–3 key features for overview; group details (2 min) | 2–3 body ideas with one example each; intro paraphrase + thesis (3 min) |
| Write | Introduction (paraphrase) → overview → detail paragraphs (14 min) | Intro → body → conclusion (30 min) |
| Check | Figures, tenses, overview present?, 150+ words (3 min) | Position clear? All parts answered? Errors? 250+ words (5 min) |

**Checking checklist (laminate this):** word count ✓ | every sentence has a verb ✓ | subject–verb agreement ✓ | articles (a/the/–) ✓ | plural -s ✓ | past vs present tense ✓ | commas in complex sentences ✓ | no contractions (Academic) ✓ | no copied question chunks ✓ | paragraphs: 3–4 (T1) / 4–5 (T2) ✓.

## 11.7 Punctuation Masterclass (the 8 rules examiners check)

1. **Every sentence ends with ONE terminal mark** (. ? !). Comma splices (*Pollution is bad, it kills fish.*) are among the most-penalised errors: split, or join with *and/but/so/because*.
2. **Commas in complex sentences:** subordinate-first → comma (*Although cities grow, housing lags.*); main-first → no comma (*Housing lags although cities grow.*).
3. **Commas with linkers:** sentence-initial linkers take a comma (*However, ... / Furthermore, ... / In contrast, ...*). Mid-sentence FANBOYS take a preceding comma (*..., but ...*).
4. **Apostrophes:** possession (*the city's growth; students' needs*) and omission (informal letters only: *don't*). NEVER for plurals (*photo's* ✗).
5. **Colons introduce lists/explanations** (*Three factors matter: cost, speed, and safety.*); **semicolons join related clauses** (*Cities grow; services lag.*) and precede *however/therefore* formally (*...grow; however, services...*).
6. **Quotation vs emphasis:** never "scare quotes" for emphasis (*"good" schools* ✗); italics/words instead.
7. **Numbers:** spell out small round numbers in essays (*three factors, half the population*); figures for data (*42%, 3.2 million, 1998*); never start a sentence with a figure (*1998 saw...* is fine in T1 — it does start with a year, legitimately).
8. **Paragraphing:** blank line between paragraphs (or indent) — every time, consistently. One-block text caps CC at 5.

## 11.9 From Band 5 to Band 7 — What Actually Changes

| Feature | Band 5 essay | Band 6 essay | Band 7 essay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position | Unclear or shifting | Generally clear | Clear throughout, well developed |
| Ideas | Few, undeveloped, repetitive | Some development, occasional irrelevance | Extended, supported, relevant |
| Organisation | Inadequate paragraphing | Generally coherent, some mechanical linking | Logical, well paragraphed, flexible cohesion |
| Vocabulary | Limited, errors impede | Adequate, some less common, errors don't impede | Sufficient range, collocations, rare slips |
| Grammar | Frequent errors, simple sentences dominate | Mix with errors, meaning mostly clear | Frequent error-free sentences, good complex range |
| Biggest single upgrade | Answer the question + paragraph properly | Add complex sentences + real examples | Vary cohesion + precise vocabulary + cut errors by half |

> Most candidates plateau at 6.0–6.5 because they write NEW essays instead of REWRITING old ones. The protocol that breaks plateaus: write → self-mark vs criteria → rewrite the same essay to Band 7+ → compare versions line by line. Chapters 12–13 give you models to rewrite toward.
