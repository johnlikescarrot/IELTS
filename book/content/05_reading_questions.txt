# 8. Reading Question Types — Techniques

This chapter teaches every Reading question type with the exact decision rules and the worked examples from the source course.

## True / False / Not Given (Facts)

These questions test **factual information** in the passage.

[[table:True / False / Not Given — the three decisions]]
| Answer | Meaning |
| TRUE | The statement says the **same** thing as the passage (possibly in different words). |
| FALSE | The statement **contradicts** (says the opposite of) the passage. |
| NOT GIVEN | The information is **not found** in the passage — there is no reference and no indication either way. |

### Worked examples (from the course)

Take this mini-passage about “Doctor X”:

<<<
*Doctor X is a professor. He teaches English at a university. He is married with three children — two sons and a daughter. He likes poetry and he likes reading a lot.*
>>>

+ **“Doctor X is an English professor.”** → **TRUE.** The words “English professor” do not appear, but “professor” + “teaches English” mean the same thing. This is paraphrase.
+ **“He has three sons.”** → **FALSE.** The passage says two sons and one daughter — the statement contradicts it.
+ **“He is into reading science fiction.”** → **NOT GIVEN.** He likes reading “a lot”, but the passage never says **what kind** of reading. You cannot say yes or no, so it is Not Given.

### Opinion vs fact (a subtle, high-band point)

+ **Facts** are generally agreed and verifiable; **opinions** are personal and debatable. They are opposites.
+ If the passage expresses something as an **opinion** (“many people **believe** the tunnel was the eighth wonder of the world”) but the statement presents it as a **fact** (“the tunnel **was** the eighth wonder of the world”), the information **contradicts** in form → **FALSE**.

### Tense matters

+ A statement in the present tense does not equal the same idea in the past. *“She **gets** up early”* is **not** the same as *“She **used to** get up early”* — “used to” means a past habit that has now finished. This difference alone can make a statement **FALSE**.

### Watch for “not given” traps

+ If a **related** word appears (e.g. “souvenirs” are mentioned) but the **specific** claim is not (whether **statues of the tunnel** were sold), the answer is **NOT GIVEN** — you have no evidence either way. Do not be seduced by a shared word.
+ Do not rush to “not given” when the passage **does** give the information in paraphrase — always scan and paraphrase before deciding (the course’s “travel by sea or land” example, where “the age of sail and horse-drawn coaches” proves the statement **TRUE**).

## Yes / No / Not Given (The Writer’s Views)

Identical logic, but about the **writer’s opinions/claims**, not facts.

[[table:Yes / No / Not Given — the three decisions]]
| Answer | Meaning |
| YES | The statement **agrees** with the writer’s view/claim. |
| NO | The statement **contradicts** the writer’s view/claim. |
| NOT GIVEN | The writer expresses **no view** on this. |

> WARNING: Follow the instructions exactly. If the question says **YES/NO/NOT GIVEN**, writing **TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN** makes the answer **wrong** (and vice versa). You may write the full words or the abbreviations Y/N/NG and T/F/NG.

### Worked examples (the “handedness” passage)

<<<
*…one in five identical twins involves one right-handed and one left-handed person, despite identical genetic material; therefore genes are not **solely** responsible for handedness… Other experts showed little interest in Marc Dax’s ideas…*
>>>

+ **“The study of twins shows that genetic determination is not the only factor in handedness.”** → **YES** (“genes are not *solely* responsible” = not the only factor).
+ **“Marc Dax’s report was widely accepted.”** → **NO** (“other experts showed **little interest**” contradicts “widely accepted”).
+ **“Dax based his findings on people who had language problems.”** → **NOT GIVEN** (the passage discusses the “functional organisation of language” but never says his subjects had language problems).
+ **“Left-handed people have more stable and successful relationships.”** → **NOT GIVEN** (no such claim appears anywhere).

## Matching Headings

You match a list of headings (usually more headings than paragraphs) to paragraphs.

+ **Read the headings first.** Understand each one; note headings that are **similar** to each other — the test will make you choose between them (e.g. “uses of spices” vs “preserving flavour” vs “spices for cooking”).
+ Then read each paragraph for the **main idea** — look for the **topic sentence**, which may be anywhere in the paragraph.
+ **Distinguish the main idea from supporting details.** A paragraph may *mention* trade routes while its main idea is “the significance of spices” — the mention is a supporting detail, not the heading.
+ The first paragraph’s heading is sometimes done for you as an example.

### Worked example (the “spices” passage)

[[table:Matching-headings answers (spices passage)]]
| Paragraph | Main idea | Heading |
| A | what spices are used for, broadly | Uses of spices |
| B | who controlled the trade over time (Muslims → Portuguese → British/Dutch) | The changing leaders |
| C | spices drove the world economy; hard to underestimate | The significance of spices |
| D/E | three uses: medicine, flavour, colour | The way spices are used |
| F | which countries produce spices (India, Vietnam, Indonesia, Iran, Sri Lanka) | The spice providers |
| G | grinding loses flavour; whole spices retain it | Preserving flavour |

## Matching People (or Names) to Statements

+ **Locate the name** in the passage (scan for capitalised names).
+ **Read what that person said** — watch for reporting verbs: *suggested, claimed, concluded, believed, argued*.
+ Match the **meaning** to the statement. There are usually **more statements than people**, and one person may be used more than once.

### Worked example (the “handedness” passage)

[[table:Matching people to findings]]
| Person | Finding |
| Brenda Milner | language is processed much more intensely in the left hemisphere (96% of people) |
| Peter Hepper | the unborn baby prefers the side developing quicker, which influences brain development |
| Michael Corballis | speech originated in gestures; the left brain (which controls the right hand) took over |

## Matching Sentence Endings

+ You are given the **first half** of a sentence and a list of possible **endings** (more endings than questions).
+ Find the first half’s **location** in the passage (scan for its keywords/synonyms), then read forward to see how the sentence logically continues.
+ The correct ending is a **grammatical and logical** match, not just a topic match.

## Completion Questions (Sentence, Summary, Notes, Table, Flow-chart)

+ The answers come **in order** within the passage.
+ **Check the word limit** (e.g. “NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS”, or “choose from the box”).
+ If a **box of words** is given, the words in the box are often paraphrased in the passage — match meaning, then check the grammar fits the gap.
+ For a **summary**, note that it usually covers **one part** of the passage, not the whole.
+ For **tables and flow-charts**, follow the logic/arrows; answers follow the passage order.

## Multiple Choice

+ The question tells you **which paragraph(s)** to read — use that.
+ Eliminate options that are **contradicted**, **partly true**, or **not mentioned**.
+ The right answer is usually a **paraphrase** of the passage; wrong answers repeat passage words in a misleading way.

## Short-Answer Questions

+ Word limit is strict (often 1–3 words).
+ The answer is a fact from the passage — a number, a name, a date — often found by scanning.
