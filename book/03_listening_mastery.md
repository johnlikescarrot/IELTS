# Chapter 3: Listening Mastery — Skills & Method

## 3.1 The Listening Test in Detail

You hear four sections once only. The recording lasts about 30 minutes; on paper you then get 10 minutes to transfer answers, while on computer you get 2 minutes to check. There are always 40 questions, one mark each — no half marks, no negative marking, so **never leave a blank**: a guess can only help.

| Section | Voices | Context | Difficulty | What it demands |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 (conversation) | Social: enquiry, booking, application, order | Easiest | Catching facts: names, numbers, dates, places |
| 2 | 1 (monologue) | Social: welcome talk, tour, instructions, radio | Easy–medium | Following descriptions, locations, steps |
| 3 | 2–4 (discussion) | Academic: students + tutor discussing coursework | Medium–hard | Tracking opinions, agreements, corrections |
| 4 | 1 (lecture) | Academic: university-style talk | Hardest | Sustained concentration, technical vocabulary |

> Video Parts 1–2 teach Sections 1–4 through real classroom exercises: the *Dubai Palm Apartments* holiday enquiry (Section 1), expedition notes (Section 1/2), the *Parks and open spaces* table with multiple choice (Section 2/3), the *Self-Access Centre* discussion (Section 3), and a business-organisation lecture (Section 4). Every technique below is demonstrated on those exercises.

## 3.2 The Three Golden Skills

### Skill 1: Prediction (before you listen)

The 20–30 seconds before each section are the highest-value time in the test. For every gap, predict the **answer type**:

- **Number only?** (price, telephone, date, quantity, percentage)
- **Spelled-out word?** (names of people/places are often spelled letter by letter)
- **Number + word?** (e.g., *37 ... Street*, *4 ... January*)
- **What kind of information?** (noun? verb? adjective? place? job? activity?)

>>> The course's Dubai Palm Apartments enquiry asks exactly these warm-up questions: which gaps need a number only, which need a spelled word, which need both, and what sort of information the other gaps need. Train yourself to answer them for every completion task.

Prediction also means grammar: *fully equipped 7 ...* needs a noun (*kitchen*); *view of 8 ...* needs a noun (*sea/lake*); *must be 9 ...* needs an adjective (*quiet/central*); *10 ... for car* needs a noun (*parking/garage*). When you know the grammar, you hear the answer slot click into place.

### Skill 2: Paraphrase recognition (while you listen)

The recording **never repeats the question wording**. It says the same thing with different words:

| Question wording | What you hear |
|---|---|
| *keep the Centre because it is an important part of their studies* | *a major part ... crucial ...* |
| *difficulty in getting help* | *hard to find staff / no one around to assist* |
| *the cost of upgrading* | *how much the improvements will set us back* |
| *fully equipped kitchen* | *everything you need for cooking* |

The lecturer annotates exactly these synonym links on screen (*major part = crucial*). Build the habit: after every practice set, list every paraphrase you missed (Chapter 2's error log). Within weeks you will own the 200 most-tested synonym pairs (they are recycled constantly).

### Skill 3: Distractor resistance (the traps)

IELTS deliberately plants wrong-but-plausible information:

1. **Corrections:** "The course starts on the 4th... no wait, the 5th of January." The second date is the answer.
2. **Rejected options:** "We could meet Monday... actually Wednesday suits everyone." Wednesday wins.
3. **Other people's answers:** A student suggests X, the tutor corrects to Y. Listen for who is authoritative.
4. **Negative qualifiers:** "Parking is available, but only for residents." Non-residents cannot use it.
5. **Similar words:** *fifteen* vs *fifty*, *P* vs *B*, *flour* vs *flower* — the test loves sound-alikes.

> If two candidate answers appear, the correct one is usually the **last one confirmed**. Hearing "no", "sorry", "actually", "on second thoughts" is your signal: update your answer.

## 3.3 Numbers, Dates, Letters & Spelling

Section 1 lives and dies on these mechanics. The course drills them explicitly (Part 1); master them cold.

### Ordinals and dates

| You hear | You write | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *first of January* | 1st January / 1 January | Any clear format is accepted |
| *the twenty-first* | 21st | Word or figure both fine within word limits |
| *two thousand and four* | 2004 | Do not write "2,004" for years |
| *the nineteen nineties* | 1990s / nineties | No apostrophe |

>>> The Dubai exercise highlights starting dates (*4 ... January*): the lecturer's on-screen note — first = 1st, second = 2nd, third = 3rd, fourth = 4th, twenty first = 21st — is your revision card. Practise writing what you hear until ordinals are automatic.

### Teen vs ty numbers

- *-teen* (13–19): stress on the second syllable (*thirTEEN*), longer vowel.
- *-ty* (20–90): stress on the first syllable (*THIRty*), short and clipped.
- In doubt, grammar and context decide: *37 2 ...* (an address) vs *37 people* — the surrounding words tell you the scale.

### The alphabet under pressure

Names and places are spelled out: *"That's Doncaster — D-O-N-C-A-S-T-E-R."* The killer pairs:

| Confused pair | How to separate them |
|---|---|
| P / B | P has a puff of air (*paper*); B is voiced (*baby*) — the course contrasts them explicitly |
| M / N | M hums with closed lips; N touches the ridge behind the teeth |
| F / S / H | F bites the lip; S hisses; H breathes (*hedge* vs *edge*) |
| G / J | G is hard (*go*); J is soft (*jam*) — often clarified as *"G for golf"* |
| E / I / A | E (*bed*) vs I (*bid*) vs A (*day*) — the classic Arab-learner trap; drill minimal pairs |
| Q / K, U / W | Q sounds like *cue*; W is *double-u* (three syllables) |

> Exam rule: if a name is spelled in the recording, copy the spelling **exactly** — even if you "know" the real spelling. Your job is transcription, not knowledge.

### Money, measurements, phone numbers

- *£15.50* → write `15.50 pounds` or `£15.50` (symbols don't count as words).
- Phone numbers: write digits as heard, including double (*double-four* = 44) and O (= 0).
- Postcodes mix letters and digits (*SW1A 4WW*) — capitals don't matter.

## 3.4 Word Limits — The Silent Killer

Instructions like **WRITE NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER** are marked by machine-like strictness:

| Instruction | *37 Lake Street* | *a fully equipped kitchen* |
|---|---|---|
| No more than 2 words + number | Correct (Lake Street = 2 words) | Wrong (4 words) |
| No more than 3 words | Correct | Wrong (4 words) |
| One word only | Wrong | Wrong |

Rules that save marks:

1. Hyphenated words (*state-of-the-art*) count as **one** word.
2. Numbers and symbols (*2004*, *£50*, *9.30am*) count as a **number**, not words.
3. Articles (*a/an/the*) and prepositions count as words — drop them when tight (*view of sea* → *sea*).
4. Plurals matter: *tree* vs *trees* is marked wrong if the recording says the other.
5. Spelling must be correct — one wrong letter = zero. (Both British and American spellings are accepted.)

! The single most common reason strong listeners lose bands: ignoring word limits. In practice, write your answer, then count aloud: "one — two — OK."

## 3.5 Section-by-Section Tactics

### Section 1: the form/note/table (Q1–10)

- Use the example time to read ahead to Q5–6 (never just Q1).
- Answers come **in order** — if you miss Q3, guess and jump to Q4; never chase backwards.
- Expect: name (spelling), address (number + street), phone (digits), date (ordinal + month), price (number + currency), requirements (nouns/adjectives).
- The Dubai Palm Apartments set is the perfect template: practise it until you score 10/10 twice in a row.

### Section 2: monologue + map/plan (Q11–20)

- For maps: memorise the compass dance — *opposite, next to, beyond, at the far end, on your left as you face...*. Trace with your finger.
- For matching (e.g., *Parks and open spaces* Q11–16): each option is paraphrased; tick possibilities lightly, confirm at the end of each item.
- Multiple choice here is easier than Section 3: usually one speaker, one pass, clear signalling (*firstly, moving on, finally*).

### Section 3: academic discussion (Q21–30)

- Identify speakers fast (usually M/F + tutor). Note who asks, who knows.
- Multiple choice: read the **stems**, not just options. Underline the difference between A/B/C (*enjoy the variety / work on their own / important part*).
- Expect heavy distractors: students propose, tutor disposes. The tutor's final position is usually the answer.
- The Self-Access Centre set (Q21–29) is your masterclass: Q21 C (*important part = major/crucial*), Q22 B (*move elsewhere*), Q23 B (*difficulty getting help*), then completion Q25–29 (*reading, CD, workbooks, timetable, ...*).

### Section 4: the lecture (Q31–40)

- No break mid-section: 8–9 minutes of continuous speech. Stamina matters — practise full sections, not fragments.
- Questions are mostly completion (notes/summary/table/flow-chart). Read the gapped text as a story: it summarises the lecture's argument.
- Technical terms are usually **given** in the questions; your answers are the everyday words around them.
- The business-culture lecture in Part 2 (*power culture, central source of power, communication as conversations not memos*) shows the level: academic framing, concrete answers.

## 3.6 Transfer Time & Answer-Sheet Discipline (Paper)

Your 10 minutes are for three jobs, in order:

1. **Transfer (6 min):** Copy answers clearly in CAPITAL LETTERS (fewer ambiguous letters). Check question numbers line up — one shifted row can cost 10 marks.
2. **Grammar-check (2 min):** Every gap: right word class? singular/plural? within word limit? spelling?
3. **Guess (2 min):** Fill every blank. Intelligent guesses (right word class, topic vocabulary) often score.

On computer, you type directly — spend your 2 minutes checking spelling and word limits only. Practise typing answers (notably capitalisation and hyphens) before test day.

## 3.7 World Accents Survival Guide

IELTS uses British, Australian, New Zealand, Canadian, and American voices — sometimes mixed within one section. The differences that cost marks:

| Feature | British (RP) | Australian/NZ | N. American |
|---|---|---|---|
| R after vowels | Dropped (*car* = "cah") | Dropped (Aus) / light (NZ) | Pronounced ("carr") |
| T between vowels | Crisp (*better*) | Flapped ("bedder", Aus) | Flapped ("bedder") |
| Short A (*dance, bath*) | Long "ah" (RP) | "eh" (NZ *pen/pin* merge!) | Flat "a" |
| Intonation | Wide range | Rising at sentence ends (Aus "uptalk") | Flatter |
| Vocabulary traps | *flat, postcode, return (ticket), bill* | *arvo, mate* (rare in tests) | *apartment, zip code, round-trip, check* |

**NZ vowel shift (the famous trap):** NZ *pen* sounds like *pin*, *bed* like *bid*. If a NZ speaker spells "...E...", double-check E vs I from context.

**Training:** one week per accent — 30 min/day of podcasts/news (BBC, ABC Australia, RNZ, CBC, NPR) + shadowing. In mocks, note the accent per section in your error log; drill your weakest accent double.

## 3.8 Concentration Training (for Sections 3–4)

Attention is trainable: Week 1 — listen to one full Section 4 daily without pausing (no phone in room). Week 2 — same + write a 3-sentence summary from memory after. Week 3 — two consecutive sections + summaries. Week 4 — full 30-min mock + transfer. Add: sleep 8h (Chapter 8's science!), no caffeine after 2 pm before study, 5-minute breathing reset between sections in practice. Exam-day: if attention breaks mid-section, anchor on the next question's keywords within 5 seconds — never rewind mentally.

## 3.10 The 10 Most-Tested Listening Traps — Drill List

1. Correction traps (*Monday... I mean Tuesday*).
2. Teen/ty confusion (*16 Salvador Street* vs *60*).
3. P/B, M/N, F/S letter confusion in spellings.
4. Word-limit violations (3 words for a 2-word gap).
5. Plural vs singular (*workbook* vs *workbooks*).
6. American vs British spelling inconsistency (*organize* vs *organise* — either accepted, but copy the recording).
7. Lost place: missing one answer then missing the next three chasing it.
8. Map orientation: left/right from the speaker's perspective, not yours.
9. Multiple-choice distractors: all three options mentioned, only one confirmed.
10. Section 4 drift: losing focus at minute 6 of a lecture.

> Drill each trap in isolation (10 items each) before you do full mocks. Chapter 5's practice sets tag every question with its trap type so you can see exactly which ones cost you marks.
