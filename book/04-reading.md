# Chapter 3 — Reading
^ Sixty minutes, forty questions, three passages: a test of location, not comprehension

The most damaging belief a candidate can hold about IELTS Reading is that it tests reading. It does not, or not in the ordinary sense. It tests your ability to **locate** specific information in a long text at speed and to **recognise it when it has been reworded**. Deep understanding of the passage is neither required nor rewarded; on the contrary, the candidate who reads for understanding runs out of time.

The course makes this point with unusual force, and returns to two words constantly: **skimming** and **scanning**.

## 3.1 The arithmetic of sixty minutes

Forty questions. Sixty minutes. **Twenty minutes per passage**, including transferring answers to the answer sheet — because unlike Listening, Reading gives you **no extra transfer time**.

| Minute | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00&ndash;0:02 | Skim passage 1 (title, first and last paragraph, first sentence of each paragraph) |
| 0:02&ndash;0:04 | Read all questions for passage 1, underline keywords |
| 0:04&ndash;0:18 | Answer, transferring as you go |
| 0:18&ndash;0:20 | Guess any remaining blanks; move on regardless |
| 0:20&ndash;0:40 | Passage 2, same pattern |
| 0:40&ndash;1:00 | Passage 3, same pattern |

Passage 3 is the hardest. Some candidates gain by doing it *second*, while they are fresh but warmed up. Experiment in practice; commit before the exam.

:::warn
**The twenty-minute rule is absolute.**
At minute twenty you move to passage 2 whether or not passage 1 is finished. Candidates who overrun on passage 1 routinely leave eight to ten questions unattempted on passage 3. Ten blank answers is a two-band penalty. Two wrong answers on passage 1 is a quarter-band penalty. The arithmetic is not close.
:::

## 3.2 Skimming and scanning: two different skills

These words are used loosely in everyday speech. In IELTS they are precise, distinct operations, and you need both.

:::key
**Skimming** = reading fast for *gist and structure*. You want to know what each paragraph is about, not what it says. Read the title, any subheadings, the first paragraph, the last paragraph, and the **first sentence of every paragraph**. Two minutes maximum. Output: a one-word note in the margin beside each paragraph — "history", "criticism", "costs", "example".
**Scanning** = searching fast for a *specific item*. Your eyes move like a search function, not like a reader. You are hunting a name, a date, a number, a capital letter, an unusual word. You do not read the surrounding sentences until you have a hit.
:::

### Why topic sentences matter so much

The course spends considerable time on the **topic sentence** — the sentence, usually first in the paragraph, that states its main idea. English academic prose is overwhelmingly built this way, and that convention is what makes twenty-minute passages possible.

Skim the first sentence of each of eight paragraphs and you have, in roughly ninety seconds, a map of the whole passage. When a question then asks about "the economic objections", you know instantly that it is paragraph E, and you scan only there.

**Exercise 3.1.** Take any IELTS passage. Cover everything except the first sentence of each paragraph. Write a two-word summary of each. Then uncover and check. If your summaries are accurate for six of eight paragraphs, your skimming is exam-ready.

:::warn
**Where topic sentences hide**
Occasionally the main idea is not in sentence one. Two patterns to watch:
**The delayed topic sentence** — paragraph opens with an anecdote or example, then states the point in sentence two or three. Signalled by: *Consider the case of...*, *In 1897, a young engineer...*
**The final topic sentence** — paragraph builds evidence and concludes with the point. Signalled by: *Thus*, *The implication is*, *What this suggests is*.
If sentence one is a narrative or a statistic, read sentence two and the last sentence before moving on.
:::

## 3.3 Paraphrase: the mechanism of the whole component

Every question in IELTS Reading is a paraphrase of something in the passage. If the question used the passage's words, the test would be a word-search. Learning to see through paraphrase is therefore the core reading skill.

Paraphrase operates through five mechanisms. Learn to recognise all five.

| Mechanism | Passage | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Synonym** | "the scheme was *abandoned*" | "the project was *discontinued*" |
| **Word-class change** | "they *decided* quickly" | "the speed of their *decision*" |
| **Voice change** | "researchers *conducted* the study" | "the study *was carried out* by researchers" |
| **Negation of the opposite** | "few scientists accepted it" | "it was *not widely* accepted" |
| **Superordinate** | "oaks, beeches and elms" | "*deciduous trees*" |

The fourth is the most dangerous in True/False/Not Given questions, because *"few accepted"* and *"not widely accepted"* look superficially different and mean exactly the same thing.

### Building paraphrase reflexes

**Exercise 3.2 — Reverse paraphrasing.** Take ten sentences from a passage. Rewrite each using no content word from the original. This is hard and slow, and it is the fastest way to build the recognition reflex. Example:

> **Original:** "Urban populations expanded rapidly during the nineteenth century, placing unprecedented strain on sanitation systems."
> **Rewritten:** "The number of people living in cities grew very quickly in the 1800s, which put more pressure than ever before on arrangements for public hygiene."

Do ten a day for three weeks. Your Reading band will move.

## 3.4 True / False / Not Given — and Yes / No / Not Given

The most feared question type, and the most misunderstood. The course drills it more than any other.

:::key
**The exact meanings**
**TRUE** — the statement agrees with the information in the passage. The passage says this, in different words.
**FALSE** — the statement contradicts the passage. The passage says the opposite.
**NOT GIVEN** — the passage neither confirms nor contradicts. The information is simply absent, or is partially there but the specific claim is not made.
**YES/NO/NOT GIVEN** is identical in mechanism but applies to the *writer's views and claims* rather than factual information. Same logic, different wording.
:::

:::warn
**The two fatal errors**
**1. Answering from world knowledge.** The question asks what *this passage* says. Not what is true. Not what you know. If the passage claims the moon is made of cheese, a statement that the moon is made of cheese is TRUE.
**2. Confusing FALSE with NOT GIVEN.** FALSE requires a *contradiction you can point to*. If you cannot underline the words that contradict the statement, it is NOT GIVEN. "The passage doesn't mention it" is the definition of NOT GIVEN, not of FALSE.
:::

### The decision procedure

1. Find the location. Statements come in **passage order** — statement 5 is after statement 4 in the text. Use this ruthlessly.
2. Read the relevant sentence and the one on each side.
3. Ask: *can I point to words that say this?* &rarr; TRUE.
4. If not: *can I point to words that say the opposite?* &rarr; FALSE.
5. If neither: &rarr; NOT GIVEN. Do not agonise. Move on.

### Worked examples

> **Passage:** "The Grand Tunnel was completed in 1843 after eighteen years of construction. Although originally intended for horse-drawn carriages, the access ramps were never built, and it was used solely by pedestrians until 1869, when it was incorporated into the London Underground railway system. It remains in use today."

| Statement | Answer | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The tunnel took less than twenty years to build. | **TRUE** | "eighteen years" — a paraphrase by numerical inference |
| Horse-drawn carriages used the tunnel before 1869. | **FALSE** | Contradicted: ramps "never built", used "solely by pedestrians" |
| The tunnel was the first of its kind in the world. | **NOT GIVEN** | Nothing about precedence anywhere |
| The tunnel is still part of the Underground network. | **TRUE** | "remains in use today" following incorporation |
| Construction of the tunnel was more expensive than planned. | **NOT GIVEN** | Cost is never mentioned |

Look at statement 2. A candidate who reads "originally intended for horse-drawn carriages" and stops will answer TRUE. The word **although** is the whole question. Concessive conjunctions — *although*, *while*, *despite*, *even though* — are where T/F/NG questions live.

### Quantifier and modality traps

Small words change truth values completely.

| Passage says | Statement says | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "some researchers argue" | "all researchers argue" | FALSE |
| "may contribute to" | "causes" | FALSE (certainty added) |
| "the largest in Europe" | "the largest in the world" | NOT GIVEN or FALSE — check scope |
| "rarely occurs" | "never occurs" | FALSE |
| "was one factor" | "was the main factor" | NOT GIVEN |
| "is likely to" | "will" | FALSE |

**Always circle:** *all, some, most, every, never, always, only, mainly, may, might, must, will, can*. These are where the answer is decided.

**Practice 3.1 — T/F/NG.**

> "Bioluminescence — the production of light by living organisms — occurs in a remarkable range of species, from fireflies to fungi, but it is in the deep ocean that it is most prevalent. Some estimates suggest that more than three-quarters of deep-sea animals are capable of producing light. The functions are varied: attracting prey, deterring predators, and signalling to mates. Dr Helena Marsh, whose team has catalogued over 400 luminescent species, cautions that the underlying chemistry is far from uniform; luciferin, the light-emitting compound, has evolved independently at least forty times."

1. Bioluminescence is found in more species on land than in the sea.
2. Most deep-sea animals can produce light.
3. Dr Marsh's team discovered 400 new species.
4. The chemistry of bioluminescence is the same across all species.
5. Bioluminescence helps some animals find food.

**Key:** 1 **FALSE** (deep ocean "most prevalent"). 2 **TRUE** ("more than three-quarters"). 3 **NOT GIVEN** — they *catalogued* 400; nothing says they *discovered* them. 4 **FALSE** ("far from uniform"). 5 **TRUE** ("attracting prey").

Question 3 is the classic NG: a number that matches, a verb that doesn't.

## 3.5 Matching Headings

You are given a list of headings (i, ii, iii...) and must assign one to each paragraph. There are always more headings than paragraphs.

:::key
**The method**
1. **Do this type last** among the question sets for that passage. By then you will have read most of the paragraphs for other questions.
2. Read all headings first. **Group them** — often two or three headings are near-synonyms, designed so that only one fits precisely.
3. Work paragraph by paragraph, not heading by heading.
4. For each paragraph, read the **first and last sentences** plus any sentence beginning with a contrast marker, then summarise the paragraph in your own words *before* looking at the headings.
5. Cross off each heading as you use it.
6. Do the paragraphs you are certain about first; the leftovers narrow the field for the difficult ones.
:::

:::warn
**A heading describes the paragraph's MAIN idea, not a detail it mentions.**
If a paragraph is about the decline of coastal fishing and mentions a 1978 government report in passing, the heading "The impact of government reports" is a trap. Ask: *if this paragraph were deleted, what would the passage lose?* That is the main idea.
:::

## 3.6 Matching Information to Paragraphs

"Which paragraph contains the following information?" Note the instruction: **any paragraph may be used more than once, or not at all.**

This is the most time-expensive type because the answers are **not in order**. Strategy: do it last, and use your skim-notes. Because you have already noted what each paragraph is about, you can go straight to two or three candidate paragraphs rather than rereading all nine.

## 3.7 Matching Features / Names

Match statements to researchers, theories, countries or periods. The names in the passage are **capitalised**, which makes them ideal scanning targets — your eye finds capital letters faster than any other feature.

Strategy: scan for each name, mark every occurrence with a letter in the margin, then read only those sentences.

Watch for: one researcher's view being *reported* by another; and names mentioned only to be dismissed.

## 3.8 Sentence, Summary, Note, Table and Flow-chart Completion

All the completion types share one rule: **answers must be words taken from the passage, exactly as they appear, within the word limit.** You may not change the form of the word.

:::warn
**You cannot "correct" the passage's grammar.**
If the passage says "the organisms *migrate*" and the sentence frame requires "the process of ____", you may not write "migrating" unless that word appears. Find a form that does appear, or reconsider your location.
:::

**Two techniques:**

1. **Predict the word class before you look.** Just as in Listening, the grammar of the gap tells you whether you need a noun, an adjective or a verb. Half of all wrong answers in this type are the right idea in the wrong word class.
2. **Completion answers are almost always in order** and almost always concentrated in one part of the passage — find the first one, and the rest are in the following paragraphs.

### Summary completion with a word box

Different rules apply. The box words are **synonyms**, not passage words. Sort the box by word class first — this typically eliminates two-thirds of the options for each gap in a single step.

## 3.9 Multiple choice, and multi-select

Same design logic as in Listening: the correct option is paraphrased; distractors reuse the passage's vocabulary with altered meaning.

For **multi-select** ("Choose TWO letters from A&ndash;E"), the correct options are usually in different paragraphs. Evaluate all five independently against the text rather than picking the two that feel best.

## 3.10 Diagram-label completion

Common in Academic Reading with a technical passage. You label parts of a device, a structure or a process.

**Strategy.** The passage describes the diagram in a *sequence* — usually spatial (bottom to top, outside in) or functional (input to output). Find the paragraph that describes the object, then read it with your finger on the diagram. The prepositions carry the marks: *beneath*, *surrounding*, *attached to*, *feeds into*, *is drawn off through*.

## 3.11 General Training Reading

Five texts across three sections, increasing in difficulty:

| Section | Content | Question focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 — Social survival** | Two or three short texts: advertisements, timetables, notices, leaflets | Factual detail, matching, T/F/NG. Speed and precision |
| **2 — Workplace survival** | Two texts: job descriptions, staff handbooks, training materials, contracts | Detail, matching, headings |
| **3 — General interest** | One long descriptive or discursive text | Full range, closest to Academic |

**The GT-specific skills:**
- **Section 1 is a data-extraction exercise.** Prices, dates, eligibility conditions, exclusions. The traps are in words like *except*, *unless*, *only if*, *excluding weekends*, *members only*.
- **Be brutally fast in Section 1.** Ten to twelve minutes for fourteen questions. Bank the time for Section 3.
- **Remember the higher band threshold** (Chapter 1.2). You need 34/40 for Band 7 versus 30 for Academic. There is no room for careless errors in the easy sections.

## 3.12 Vocabulary in Reading: what to do with unknown words

You will not know every word. You do not need to.

:::key
**The three-step unknown-word protocol**
**1. Is it load-bearing?** If the word is not in or near an answer, skip it entirely. Most unknown words are irrelevant.
**2. If it is, use morphology.** Prefix, root, suffix. *In-* (not), *re-* (again), *sub-* (under), *-tion* (noun, process), *-ity* (noun, quality), *-ous* (adjective), *-ify* (verb, to make).
**3. Use the sentence.** Contrast markers (*but, however, whereas*) tell you the word means the opposite of something nearby. Lists tell you it belongs to a category. Appositives ("X, that is, a kind of Y") define it outright.
:::

**Example.** "Unlike the gregarious starling, the shrike is a solitary hunter." You may not know *gregarious*. *Unlike* + *solitary* tells you it means the opposite of solitary: sociable, living in groups. That is enough.

## 3.13 Transfer, spelling and the answer sheet

- **Spelling counts.** A correctly located answer spelled wrongly scores zero.
- **Copy exactly**, including capitals for proper nouns.
- **Write answers on the sheet as you go**, passage by passage. Do not leave transfer to the end; candidates who do lose whole passages to the clock.
- **Number carefully.** A single misalignment can invalidate a dozen answers.
- **Never leave a blank.** For T/F/NG, guessing gives you a one-in-three chance. For matching, one in five or six. Free expected marks.

## 3.14 A four-week Reading training programme

**Week 1 — Untimed accuracy.** Do full passages with no clock. Aim for 90% accuracy. Then, for every wrong answer, find the exact words in the passage that produce the right one and write out the paraphrase relationship. This is the core learning activity.

**Week 2 — Skimming and structure.** Take five passages a day. Do *not* answer questions. Skim each in two minutes and write a one-line summary of each paragraph. Then check by reading properly. You are training the topic-sentence reflex.

**Week 3 — Timed by passage.** Twenty minutes per passage, hard stop. Accept a dip in accuracy; it will recover.

**Week 4 — Full tests.** Sixty minutes, three passages, transfer included, no exceptions.

### The reading you do outside practice tests

Read non-fiction prose in English for thirty minutes daily: quality newspapers, popular science magazines, long-form journalism. Do not look up every word. The purpose is not vocabulary acquisition; it is building the *reading speed* and the *tolerance for uncertainty* that a timed test demands.

## 3.15 Reading diagnostic checklist

- I skimmed before reading any question.
- I noted a one-word topic for each paragraph.
- I underlined the keywords in every question.
- I found the answer's location before deciding the answer.
- For T/F/NG, I could point to specific words for every TRUE and every FALSE.
- I did not answer from general knowledge.
- I circled all quantifiers and modal verbs in the statements.
- I moved to the next passage at the twenty-minute mark.
- I transferred answers passage by passage.
- I left no blanks.
