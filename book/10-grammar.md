# Chapter 9 — Grammar for Range and Accuracy
^ The structures that raise bands, and the errors that hold them down

Grammatical Range and Accuracy is 25% of both Writing and Speaking — a full eighth of your overall band. The criterion has two halves that pull in opposite directions, and the course teaches them in exactly the right order: first learn the three sentence types, then learn what to do inside each.

## 9.1 The three sentence types

This is the backbone of the grammar teaching in the course, and the message is blunt: an answer made of simple sentences "will not give you more than Band 5."

### Simple sentences

**One independent clause.** One subject, one finite verb, a complete thought.

> *The internet has transformed education.*
> *Pollution levels rose sharply after 1995.*

Simple sentences are not bad. A short simple sentence after two long complex ones creates emphasis and rhythm. But an essay built only from them demonstrates no range.

### Compound sentences

**Two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction.** Each half could stand alone.

The seven coordinators are remembered as **FANBOYS** — a mnemonic the course uses explicitly:

| Letter | Conjunction | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F** | for | reason (formal, literary) |
| **A** | and | addition |
| **N** | nor | negative addition |
| **B** | but | contrast |
| **O** | or | alternative |
| **Y** | yet | contrast (despite expectation) |
| **S** | so | result |

:::key
**Punctuation rule: a comma goes BEFORE the FANBOYS conjunction when it joins two independent clauses.**
*The internet offers enormous benefits, **but** it also carries significant risks.*
No comma when the conjunction joins two verbs with one subject:
*The internet offers enormous benefits **but** carries significant risks.*
:::

**Building a compound sentence — worked example from the course:**

> Simple 1: *The internet has many advantages.*
> Simple 2: *The internet has some drawbacks.*
> Compound: *The internet has many advantages, **but** it also has some drawbacks.*

Note: the second clause needs its own subject. Writing *"The internet has many advantages, but the internet has some drawbacks"* is repetitive; the pronoun **it** is required. This exact correction appears in the classroom.

:::warn
**Comma splice** — joining two independent clauses with only a comma — is an error at every band.
✗ *The internet has many advantages, it also has drawbacks.*
✓ *The internet has many advantages, but it also has drawbacks.* (coordinator)
✓ *The internet has many advantages; it also has drawbacks.* (semicolon)
✓ *The internet has many advantages. It also has drawbacks.* (full stop)
✓ *Although the internet has many advantages, it also has drawbacks.* (subordination)
:::

### Complex sentences

**One independent clause plus at least one dependent (subordinate) clause.** The dependent clause cannot stand alone.

> *Although the internet has many advantages, it also carries risks.*
> *Students who receive early support are more likely to complete their studies.*
> *If governments invested more in prevention, healthcare costs would fall.*

The course spends real time on the distinction, because students find it slippery:

:::key
**How to test for a dependent clause**
Read the clause aloud on its own. Does it feel finished?
*"He studied hard"* — finished. Independent.
*"Although he studied hard"* — unfinished; you are waiting for something. Dependent.
The subordinating word (*although, if, because, when, who, which, that...*) is what makes it dependent. Remove it and the clause becomes independent.
:::

### The subordinators

| Relationship | Subordinators |
| --- | --- |
| **Contrast/concession** | although, though, even though, whereas, while |
| **Reason** | because, since, as, given that, in that |
| **Purpose** | so that, in order that |
| **Condition** | if, unless, provided that, as long as, in case |
| **Time** | when, while, before, after, until, as soon as, once, by the time |
| **Result** | so ... that, such ... that |
| **Manner** | as, as if, as though |
| **Place** | where, wherever |
| **Relative** | who, whom, whose, which, that, where, when |

:::warn
**A dependent clause alone is a sentence fragment.**
✗ *Although the policy was expensive.*
✓ *Although the policy was expensive, it proved effective.*
Fragments are marked as errors and are common in candidates who are trying to use subordination for the first time.
:::

**Comma rule for complex sentences:** if the dependent clause comes **first**, use a comma. If it comes **second**, usually no comma.
*Although it rained, we went out.* / *We went out although it rained.*

### Compound-complex

Two independent clauses plus at least one dependent. Useful occasionally; do not build a whole essay from them.

> *Although online learning offers flexibility, many students struggle with isolation, and completion rates remain stubbornly low.*

## 9.2 Concession structures: despite, in spite of, although, however

The course devotes an extended sequence to this cluster, because students systematically confuse them. The rule is purely grammatical: **what follows each one?**

| Expression | Followed by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **although / even though / though** | a **clause** (subject + verb) | *Although he studied hard, he failed.* |
| **despite / in spite of** | a **noun, pronoun or -ing form** | *Despite studying hard, he failed.* / *Despite his hard work, he failed.* |
| **despite the fact that / in spite of the fact that** | a **clause** | *Despite the fact that he studied hard, he failed.* |
| **however / nevertheless / nonetheless** | a **new sentence** (or after a semicolon) | *He studied hard. However, he failed.* / *He studied hard; however, he failed.* |
| **but / yet** | a **clause**, after a comma | *He studied hard, but he failed.* |
| **whereas / while** | a **clause** (contrasting two things) | *Whereas urban areas grew, rural areas declined.* |

:::warn
**The three commonest errors in this cluster**
✗ *Despite he studied hard, he failed.* → **despite + noun/-ing**, never a clause.
✓ *Despite studying hard...* / *Despite the fact that he studied hard...*
✗ *Although he studied hard, but he failed.* → never use **although** and **but** together. One subordinator per relationship.
✗ *He studied hard, however, he failed.* → **however** cannot join two clauses with commas. This is a comma splice.
✓ *He studied hard; however, he failed.* / *He studied hard. However, he failed.*
:::

**Transformation drill.** Express the same idea five ways:

1. *Although John studied hard, he did not achieve a high score.*
2. *John studied hard; however, he did not achieve a high score.*
3. *Despite studying hard, John did not achieve a high score.*
4. *In spite of his hard work, John did not achieve a high score.*
5. *John studied hard, yet he did not achieve a high score.*

Being able to produce all five on demand is worth a great deal in both Writing and Speaking. Practise until it is instant.

## 9.3 Relative clauses

The highest-value complex structure, because they let you add information without starting a new sentence.

| Type | Punctuation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Defining** (identifies which one) | **No commas** | *Students **who miss the deadline** will be penalised.* |
| **Non-defining** (adds extra information) | **Commas** | *My brother, **who lives in Dubai**, is a pharmacist.* |

**The relative pronouns:** *who* (people), *which* (things), *that* (people or things, defining only), *whose* (possession), *where* (places), *when* (times), *why* (reasons).

:::key
**Three upgrades worth learning**
**1. Quantifier + of which/whom:** *The survey questioned 400 residents, **most of whom** reported dissatisfaction.*
**2. Preposition + which (formal):** *This is the framework **within which** the policy operates.*
**3. Reduced relative clauses:** drop *who/which + be*.
*The policies **which were introduced** in 2010* → *The policies **introduced** in 2010.*
*Students **who are living** abroad* → *Students **living** abroad.*
Reduced relatives are compact, sophisticated and very common in academic prose.
:::

:::warn
- Do not use **that** in a non-defining clause. ✗ *My brother, that lives in Dubai...*
- Do not double the subject. ✗ *The man who he called me...* ✓ *The man who called me...*
- **Whose** is for possession by people *and* things: *a policy whose effects are unclear.*
:::

## 9.4 Conditionals

Essential for Task 2 (speculation, recommendation) and Part 3 Speaking.

| Type | Form | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Zero** | If + present, present | General truth | *If temperatures rise, ice melts.* |
| **First** | If + present, will/may/might + inf | Real future possibility | *If governments act now, emissions will fall.* |
| **Second** | If + past, would/could/might + inf | Hypothetical present/future | *If governments invested more, emissions would fall.* |
| **Third** | If + past perfect, would have + pp | Unreal past | *If they had acted earlier, the crisis would have been avoided.* |
| **Mixed** | If + past perfect, would + inf | Past condition, present result | *If they had acted earlier, we would not be in this position.* |

**Alternatives to "if" — range markers:**
- *Unless action is taken...* (= if ... not)
- *Provided that / As long as funding continues...*
- *Should governments fail to act...* (inversion, formal — no "if")
- *Were the policy adopted...* (inversion of second conditional)
- *Had they acted sooner...* (inversion of third conditional)
- *In the event of a downturn...*
- *Suppose that...*

The three inverted forms are strong Band 8 signals. Learn one and use it once.

## 9.5 Passive voice

Essential for Task 1 process diagrams, useful throughout academic writing where the agent is unimportant.

**Form:** be + past participle.

| Tense | Active | Passive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present simple | They collect the data | The data **is collected** |
| Past simple | They collected the data | The data **was collected** |
| Present perfect | They have collected | The data **has been collected** |
| Past perfect | They had collected | The data **had been collected** |
| Future | They will collect | The data **will be collected** |
| Modal | They must collect | The data **must be collected** |
| -ing | — | *After **being collected**, the data...* |
| Perfect participle | — | *Having been collected, the data is then...* |

**Use the passive when:** the agent is unknown, obvious or irrelevant; you want to keep the topic at the front of the sentence; you are describing a process.

:::warn
**Do not overuse it.** Whole essays in the passive read as evasive and are harder to follow. Roughly one passive in five or six sentences is natural in academic prose. In a process diagram description, the proportion is far higher — that is the exception.
:::

## 9.6 Tenses: the ones that matter

You do not need all twelve. You need these six used accurately and a couple of others for range.

| Tense | Use in IELTS | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Present simple** | General truths, habits, chart descriptions | *The graph shows...*; *I work as a pharmacist.* |
| **Present continuous** | Current temporary situations, changing trends | *More people are working remotely.* |
| **Present perfect** | Action with present relevance; unfinished time | *Technology **has transformed** education.* |
| **Present perfect continuous** | Duration up to now | *I **have been studying** English for six years.* |
| **Past simple** | Finished past actions, completed data periods | *Arrivals **rose** between 1995 and 2010.* |
| **Past continuous** | Background action interrupted | *I **was living** in Cairo when I first took the test.* |
| **Past perfect** | Earlier past | *By 2005, numbers **had already doubled**.* |
| **Future forms** | Predictions, projections | *Figures **are projected to** reach...* |

:::key
**The present perfect, explained properly — because it is the tense the course spends most time correcting.**
Use *have/has + past participle* to talk about an action **without specifying when**, where the result matters now.
*I **have bought** a car.* — I own a car now. The time is irrelevant.
*I **bought** a car **last week**.* — A specific finished time. Present perfect is impossible here.
✗ *I have bought a car last week.* — **Never** combine present perfect with a finished time expression.
Signal words for present perfect: *ever, never, already, yet, just, so far, recently, since, for, over the past decade.*
Signal words for past simple: *yesterday, last year, in 2010, ago, when I was young.*
:::

## 9.7 Articles: a, an, the, zero

The commonest error in the scripts of otherwise strong candidates, especially those whose first language has no article system.

| Use | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **a / an** | Singular countable, first mention, non-specific | *I saw **a** film last night.* |
| **the** | Specific, already mentioned, unique, superlative, defined by a phrase | *The film I saw was excellent.* |
| **the** | Unique things | *the government, the environment, the internet, the sun* |
| **zero** | Plural or uncountable generalisations | *Children learn quickly.* / *Education is a right.* |
| **the** | Specific group | *The children in my class learn quickly.* |

:::key
**The generalisation rule, which fixes half of all article errors:**
To generalise about a category, use **plural with no article**.
✓ *Cars cause pollution.*
✗ *The cars cause pollution.* (means specific cars)
✗ *Car causes pollution.* (ungrammatical)
For uncountable generalisations, also no article: *Pollution is a problem.* Not *The pollution is a problem* unless you mean specific pollution.
:::

**Fixed expressions to memorise:** *in the morning / at night*; *go to school / to the school* (as a building); *play the piano / play football*; *in hospital (BrE) / in the hospital (AmE)*; *the rich, the poor, the elderly*; *by car, on foot*; *the number of X is / a number of X are*.

## 9.8 Subject–verb agreement

| Trap | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intervening phrase | *The **number** of students **are** rising* | *The **number** ... **is** rising* |
| "A number of" | *A number of students **is**...* | *A number of students **are**...* |
| Uncountables | *Informations are...* | *Information **is**...* |
| Collective nouns | — | *The government **has** decided* (BrE also allows *have*) |
| Each/every | *Each of the students **have**...* | *Each of the students **has**...* |
| Neither/either | *Neither of them **are**...* | *Neither of them **is**...* (formal) |
| There is/are | *There **is** many reasons* | *There **are** many reasons* |
| Percentage | — | *40% of the population **is/are**...* (depends on the noun) |

## 9.9 Countable and uncountable nouns

Uncountable nouns take no plural -s and no *a/an*. The list that causes almost all IELTS errors:

**information, advice, research, knowledge, equipment, furniture, luggage, traffic, money, news, progress, evidence, work, accommodation, transport, staff, weather, homework, software, feedback, damage, behaviour, pollution**

✗ *informations, advices, researches, equipments, furnitures, an advice*
✓ *a piece of information, a piece of advice, a piece of research, an item of equipment*

**Quantifiers that go with each:**

| Countable | Uncountable | Both |
| --- | --- | --- |
| many, few, a few, fewer, several, a number of | much, little, a little, less, a great deal of, an amount of | some, any, a lot of, plenty of, most, all, no |

*Fewer cars* (countable) but *less traffic* (uncountable).

## 9.11 Punctuation

| Mark | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| **Full stop** | Ends a sentence. If you are unsure about a long sentence, break it. |
| **Comma** | After an introductory phrase or clause; before FANBOYS joining independent clauses; around non-defining relative clauses; separating list items. **Never** joining two independent clauses alone. |
| **Semicolon** | Joins two closely related independent clauses; before a conjunctive adverb (*; however,*). Use once or twice per essay, correctly. |
| **Colon** | Introduces a list, explanation or elaboration. *There is one solution: investment.* |
| **Apostrophe** | Possession (*the government's policy*, *students' results*). **Not** for plurals. Avoid contractions entirely in Task 1 and 2. |
| **Capital letters** | Sentence starts, proper nouns, nationalities, languages, months, days. Not for emphasis. |

## 9.12 Ten structures to make automatic

Practise these until you can produce them without thinking. Each is a range marker.

1. **Not only ... but also (with inversion):** *Not only does remote work reduce commuting, but it also widens access to employment.*
2. **Cleft sentence:** *What matters most is not the technology itself but how it is used.*
3. **Reduced relative:** *Policies introduced in the last decade have had mixed results.*
4. **Perfect participle:** *Having reviewed the evidence, the committee recommended reform.*
5. **Inverted conditional:** *Were governments to invest more, outcomes would improve.*
6. **The more ... the more:** *The more accessible education becomes, the greater the economic return.*
7. **Concessive fronting:** *While the costs are considerable, the long-term benefits are greater.*
8. **Passive with modal:** *Greater emphasis should be placed on prevention.*
9. **Noun phrase subject:** *The rapid expansion of urban populations has placed pressure on infrastructure.*
10. **Conditional with unless:** *Unless significant action is taken, the trend is likely to continue.*

Write each one about five different topics. Twenty minutes a day for a week and they become part of your active grammar.
