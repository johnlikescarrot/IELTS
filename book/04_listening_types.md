# Chapter 4: Listening — Every Question Type, Worked

## 4.1 How to Use This Chapter

Each of the six Listening task families is presented the same way: **what it looks like → step-by-step method → worked example from the video course → practice drills**. Cover the answers, attempt each example, then compare your reasoning — not just your answer — with the walkthrough.

## 4.2 Form Completion (Section 1)

**Looks like:** a form with labelled fields and numbered gaps (name, address, phone, dates, requirements). Instruction: *Write no more than TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER.*

**Method:**

1. Classify every gap (number only / spelled word / number + word / information type).
2. Note the units printed beside gaps (*euros, January, Street*) — never repeat them in your answer.
3. Listen for the field label's paraphrase (*"What's the name for the booking?"* = Name).
4. For spelled names: write letters as you hear, then assemble at the end.
5. Check: capitals for names, digits for phones, ordinals for dates.

>>> Worked example — Dubai Palm Apartments (Video Part 1). A man phones about a holiday apartment; Amanda takes the enquiry. Gap 1 (Name): you hear the name spelled — write exactly what you hear. Gap 2 (Address: 37 ...): number + street name; the recording says the street twice, once spelled. Gap 3 (Telephone): digits with "double" and "O" — write figures. Gap 4 (Starting date: ... January): ordinal + month; trap: the caller first mentions a different month, then corrects to January — the correction is the rule (Chapter 3). Gap 6 (Price: maximum ... euros): number only; trap: two prices mentioned (standard vs maximum) — the question asks for the maximum. Gaps 7–10 test grammar prediction: equipped ... (noun: kitchen), view of ... (noun), must be ... (adjective), ... for car (noun: parking).

**Drills:**

1. Transcribe 10 spelled names from any Listening Section 1 (pause after each spelling, then check).
2. Write 20 dates from dictation (mix ordinals: 1st–31st) until error-free.
3. Re-do the Dubai-style set with a 2-word limit enforced — recount every answer aloud.

## 4.3 Note & Table Completion (Sections 1–4)

**Looks like:** headed notes or a table with gaps. Notes follow the recording's order; tables may require you to track rows *and* columns.

**Method:**

1. Read the headings — they tell you the recording's structure (e.g., *Name / Age / Occupation / Expeditions / Skills / Qualifications / Free-time*).
2. Predict each gap's word class from its sentence (*has crossed 4 ...* → a noun: desert/river; *degree in 8 ...* → a subject: biology/law).
3. In tables, fix your place by the **filled cells** — they are anchors (*Holt Island ... many different 11 ...* → plural noun: *trees*; *between 12 ... and Sunday* → day: *Friday/Saturday*).
4. Beware column traps: the answer for "Open" vs "Of particular interest" — know which column each gap sits in.

>>> Worked example A — Expedition notes (Video Parts 1–2). A couple interview a traveller (Sanjay) for a mountain expedition. Classification warm-up (exactly as the lecturer teaches): which gaps need a number (Age), a spelled word (surname), a place (crossed ...), a subject/language (degree in ..., can speak ...), a free-time activity (... , keeping ...), a job (occupation)? Gap 2 (Age: ...): number — trap: two ages mentioned (his age vs years of experience). Gap 5 (Mount ...): spelled mountain name. Gap 6 (... course): adjective + course (first-aid/survival) — within "two words and/or a number", "first aid" counts as two words: allowed. Gap 10 (keeping ...): the -ing frame predicts an animal/hobby noun (bees/fit — recording decides).

>>> Worked example B — Parks and open spaces table + multiple choice (Video Part 1, Q11–16). Table gaps: Q11 (many different ... trees — wait, the frame is "many different 11 ...": answer "trees" is printed? No — in the lesson the lecturer reveals answers progressively: 11 trees, 12 Friday, Sunday, 13 farm). Learn the move: Q12–13 (between ... and ...; reconstruction of a 2,000-year-old ... farm) are solved by tracking the row (Holt Island; Longfield Country Park) while the speaker lists details in column order. Q14–16 (multiple choice: Monday/Wednesday/Saturday activities) follow the table — when the format changes mid-section, lift your eyes early during the 30-second pause and pre-read the new stems.

**Drills:**

1. Take any table task and cover the answers: narrate the speaker's path (row by row) from the tapescript.
2. Build 5 grammar frames (*has done a ... course / can speak ... / degree in ...*) and brainstorm 3 possible answers each — then listen to see which came.
3. Practise "anchor recovery": deliberately miss a table answer, then relocate using the next filled cell within 5 seconds.

## 4.4 Sentence & Summary Completion (Sections 2–4)

**Looks like:** numbered sentences with a gap at the start, middle, or end, testing one idea each. Often introduced by *Write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS* (Section 3) or *THREE WORDS* (Section 4 varies).

**Method:**

1. Read each sentence as a proposition with a hole — understand the sentence *before* you listen.
2. Underline the **locator words** (rare nouns/verbs that will be paraphrased, not repeated).
3. Listen for the *idea*, then fit the exact words to the gap's grammar.
4. End-gaps are easiest (the sentence builds to them); start-gaps are hardest (the answer comes before you orient) — steal extra seconds for start-gaps.

>>> Worked example — Self-Access Centre improvements (Video Part 2, Q25–29). Q25 (The level of the ... reading materials ... should be more clearly shown): the discussion buries this in complaints about labelling levels ("label them more clearly" — the tapescript phrase). Answer: reading. Q26 (Update the ... collection): the recording lists resources (books, tapes, CDs...) then selects one for updating — track the verb update, not the nouns. Answer: CD. Q27 (Buy some ... and divide them up): plural noun after "some" — workbooks; trap: singular/plural (the recording clearly pluralises). Q28 (organise a ... for supervising): timetable/rota — paraphrase of "agreement on who supervises when". Q29 (Install an ...): the security item from the discussion's final turn.

**Drills:**

1. Locator drill: for 10 sentences, list 2 locator words each and predict their paraphrases before listening.
2. Grammar-fit drill: given 10 gaps with frames, write the required word class (noun/verb/adjective/number) in 60 seconds.
3. Tapescript paraphrase hunt: highlight every synonym link for one full Section 3 (aim: 15+ pairs).

## 4.5 Multiple Choice (Sections 2–4)

**Looks like:** a stem + 3 options (A/B/C), or "choose TWO letters from five" (A–E). Single-answer MC dominates Sections 2–3; list-selection appears in Sections 2–4.

**Method (single answer):**

1. Read stems carefully; skim options only for their **differences** (verbs and objects differ — that's what the recording will separate).
2. Expect all three options to be mentioned. Your job: track which one is **confirmed** (agreed, chosen, true) vs merely discussed.
3. Mark as you listen: cross out rejected options, circle the confirmed one. Decide by the question's end — never revisit.
4. Beware the "half-right" option: true statement, wrong question (e.g., true of teachers, but the question asks about students).

**Method (choose two):** Treat as two independent true/false judgments. Five options, two correct: eliminate with prejudice — cross out the moment an option is contradicted.

>>> Worked example — Self-Access Centre opinions (Video Part 2, Q21–24). Q21 (Students want to keep the Centre because...): A (variety of equipment) is mentioned but dismissed; B (work on their own/alone) is raised by one speaker; C (important part of their studies = major/crucial) is the agreed reason — answer C. The lesson: agreement language ("exactly", "that's the main thing") marks answers. Q22 (Some teachers would prefer to...): A (close) is an extreme nobody supports; C (restrict access) is floated then dropped; B (move elsewhere) is the teachers' actual preference. Q23 (students' main concern about the library): A (size) vs B (difficulty getting help) vs C (lack of materials) — "main" means the concern that gets repeated and agreed. Q24 (Director of Studies concerned about...): cost vs space — follow the Director's turns, not the students'.

**Drills:**

1. Difference-spotting: for 10 MC questions, write the A/B/C difference in 5 words before listening.
2. Agreement-language list: from tapescripts, collect 20 confirmation phrases (*exactly, that's right, the main thing is, we've agreed...*) and 20 rejection phrases (*but, however, the problem is, I'm not so sure...*).
3. Choose-two elimination: practise crossing out 3 wrong options in real time (pause allowed first, then live).

## 4.6 Matching (Sections 2–4)

**Looks like:** a list of items (people, places, features) matched to a list of options (opinions, facts, categories). Boxes, programmes, or "who said what".

**Method:**

1. Learn the **options** cold (they repeat across items); skim the items lightly.
2. Items come in order; options come in any order and may repeat (check instructions: *You may use any letter more than once*?).
3. Listen for item identifiers (names stressed, order preserved), then judge the option immediately.
4. If you miss an item, sacrifice it — the next identifier is your lifeline.

**Drills:**

1. Option-memorisation: cover options after 20 seconds and recite them (A–F) before the recording starts.
2. Identifier tracking: from a tapescript, highlight every item identifier and note how each is introduced.
3. Do 3 matching sets back-to-back to build the "judge-and-move-on" reflex.

## 4.7 Map / Plan / Diagram Labelling (Section 2)

**Looks like:** a labelled map/plan with lettered gaps (A–H) or a diagram with numbered stages. Instruction: *Label the plan below. Choose ... from the box* or *Write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS.*

**Method:**

1. Orient first: find the entrance, the compass (if any), and the "you are here".
2. Learn the letter positions (A = ?, B = ? ...) so you can write letters without looking down.
3. Follow the speaker's journey with your finger; answers come in route order.
4. Master the language of location (below) until it is instant.

| Meaning | Phrases you will hear |
|---|---|
| Opposite | across from, facing, on the far side from |
| Next to | beside, alongside, adjacent to, right next to |
| Between | in the middle of, halfway between |
| Beyond/past | just past, further along, at the far end of |
| Near entrance | just inside, on your right as you enter |
| Corner | in the top/bottom left-hand corner |

> Left/right traps: "on your left as you face the lake" — always take the **speaker's** perspective. When in doubt, the next location confirms the last: use it to self-correct.

**Drills:**

1. Blank-map narration: describe a route around your home using 10 location phrases, recorded.
2. Letter-speed: label 3 maps with letters only (no words) to practise eyes-up listening.
3. One district map, three journeys: listen once per journey, labelling different gap sets.

## 4.8 Mixed Sets & Format Changes

Harder sections switch format mid-stream (table → multiple choice; notes → matching). The 30-second pauses are repositioning time:

1. Finish the old format's last answer (guess if needed) in 5 seconds.
2. Spend 25 seconds pre-reading the NEW format's first 3–4 questions.
3. Note the new instruction (word limit? letter choice?) — format changes often change limits.

! Most "I got lost" disasters happen at format changes, not hard vocabulary. Practise the 25-second pivot until it is a reflex (Chapter 5's sets include two mid-section format changes).

## 4.9 Fully Worked Examples II (Advanced Sets)

>>> FULL WORKED EXAMPLE — Section 2 map: Riverside Country Market (Video-style). You hear: "Welcome to Riverside, and let me help you find your way around. As you come through the main entrance on Mill Street, the information kiosk is immediately on your right — that's A on your map. Directly opposite the kiosk, across the courtyard, is the covered food hall — B. Now, if you walk past the food hall toward the river, you'll see the craft stalls on your left, just before the old bridge — that's C. The bridge itself is closed for repairs, so to reach the garden centre — D — you need to go back and follow the riverside path east for about 200 metres; it's the big glass building you can't miss. Finally, the car park — E — is behind the garden centre, accessed from River Lane, not from Mill Street." Method on display: orientation first (entrance + street names as anchors); route order (A→B→C→D→E follows the speaker's journey); left/right from the speaker's walking perspective ("on your left" as you walk toward the river); the trap (bridge closed — the garden centre is NOT across the bridge; the car park's access road differs from the market entrance). Answers: A information kiosk, B food hall, C craft stalls, D garden centre, E car park.

>>> FULL WORKED EXAMPLE — Choosing TWO letters (Section 3/4 style). "Which TWO advantages of urban beekeeping does the speaker mention? A higher honey prices, B improved crop pollination, C lower hive maintenance, D reduced pesticide use nearby, E stronger bee colonies." Tapescript: "...and there are real benefits. For a start, city gardens produce far more fruit when hives are nearby — pollination improves dramatically. Some people claim urban honey sells for more, but actually prices are about the same as rural honey. What is true is that several boroughs have cut pesticide spraying in parks to protect the hives — a genuine side benefit. Maintenance, I'm afraid, is heavier in cities, not lighter, and colonies face just as many diseases." Answers: B (pollination) + D (reduced pesticides). A mentioned but denied; C contradicted; E contradicted. The drill: judge each option the moment it is addressed — cross out, tick, move on.

>>> FULL WORKED EXAMPLE — Section 4 summary completion (Video-style business lecture). Gapped summary: "The speaker defines organisational culture as shared 31 ... and values. In a power culture, control rests with 32 ..., and communication happens through 33 ... rather than formal meetings." Tapescript (from the course's Section 4 lecture, Part 2): "...we often refer to these social characteristics as the culture of the organisation. This includes its beliefs, values and things like that... The first type is called the power culture... a central source of power... communication usually takes the form of conversations rather than, say, formal meetings or written memos." Answers: 31 beliefs, 32 one person / a central source (accept: central source of power → within word limit: "central source"), 33 conversations. Skills: the definition sentence holds Q31 (beliefs + values — values printed? no — gap is beliefs); Q32 needs the power-holder (one person / central source); Q33 contrasts with formal meetings (conversations). Note the classic Section 4 frame: term defined → example → contrast.

## 4.10 Ten One-Minute Distractor Drills

Read each mini-script, predict the answer, then check. (Answers: 1 Tuesday; 2 60; 3 garage; 4 B; 5 False-test — "postgraduate"; 6 1998; 7 Wednesday; 8 £42; 9 bees; 10 platform 2.)

1. "We meet Monday... actually, can we make it Tuesday? Monday's a holiday." (Q: meeting day?)
2. "It costs sixteen... no, sixty pounds — sorry, six-zero." (Q: price?)
3. "Parking? There's street parking, but residents only. Visitors use the garage on Level B." (Q: visitor parking?)
4. "A morning flight suits me — oh, but the afternoon one is direct. Let's take the afternoon." (Q: chosen flight?)
5. "The course is open to undergraduates... and postgraduates too, actually — everyone's welcome." (Q: "Only undergraduates may enrol" — True/False/NG logic.)
6. "I was born in nineteen ninety-eight... my brother in ninety-five." (Q: speaker's birth year?)
7. "The library closes Wednesday at five — Friday at eight." (Q: Wednesday closing?) 8. "Standard delivery is £30; express is £42." (Q: express cost?)
9. "Keeping chickens? No — bees. We keep bees." (Q: keeping...?)
10. "The Edinburgh train leaves from platform 2... the Glasgow one from platform 5." (Q: Edinburgh platform?)
