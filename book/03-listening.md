# Chapter 2 — Listening
^ Thirty minutes, forty questions, one play: the component where technique beats fluency

Listening is the component where a candidate with modest English can outperform a fluent speaker who has not learned the system. The recording plays **once**. There is no rewind. Everything therefore depends on being in the right place, at the right moment, expecting the right kind of word.

The course devotes its first two sessions almost entirely to this component, and the emphasis is unmistakable: *instructions first, prediction second, everything else third*.

## 2.1 The architecture of the test

| Section | Context | Speakers | Typical difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Part 1** | Everyday social transaction: booking, enquiry, registration | 2 speakers | Easiest. Names, numbers, dates, addresses |
| **Part 2** | Monologue on a social topic: a tour, a facility, an announcement | 1 speaker | Maps, plans, matching |
| **Part 3** | Educational/training discussion: a tutorial, a project meeting | 2&ndash;4 speakers | Hardest. Opinions, disagreement, multiple choice |
| **Part 4** | Academic lecture on any subject | 1 speaker | Dense. Note/summary completion, no break mid-part |

Ten questions per part. The recording moves strictly forward through the questions — question 7 is always answered after question 6. If you miss one, you have lost one mark, not the section. **Let it go immediately.** The single most expensive habit in Listening is continuing to hunt for question 6 while questions 7, 8 and 9 go past.

:::warn
**The cascade failure**
Missing one answer is worth one mark. Missing one answer and then losing your place is worth five. The instant you realise you have missed something, move your pencil to the next question and look at it. Recovery beats retrieval, every time.
:::

## 2.2 Instructions are part of the test

The course opens its listening practice not with a recording but with a line of text, and spends several minutes on it:

> The instructions are saying what? "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER." So in this case your answer may take several forms.

That phrase — *and/or* — is doing real work. Unpack the possibilities:

| Instruction | Permitted answers |
| --- | --- |
| ONE WORD ONLY | `station` |
| ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER | `station`, `14`, `platform 14` |
| NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS | `bus station`, `station` |
| NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER | `bus station`, `14 Mill Road`, `station 14`, `14` |
| NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER | `the old bus station`, `3 September 1994` |

Three rules govern the counting, and they decide marks:

1. **Hyphenated compounds count as one word.** `part-time` is one word. `air-conditioning` is one word.
2. **Numbers written as digits count as a number, not a word.** `25` is a number; `twenty-five` is one word. Write digits — they are faster and safer.
3. **Articles count.** `the bus station` is three words. If the limit is two, write `bus station`. Grammatical articles are almost never required for the answer to be correct.

:::warn
**Over the limit scores zero, even when the meaning is perfect.**
Limit: TWO WORDS. Recording: "...you'll need to bring a valid student identity card."
Correct: `student card` or `identity card`.
Marked wrong: `a student identity card` (four words), `student identity card` (three words).
The candidate understood everything and scored nothing. This is the cheapest mark in IELTS to lose and the easiest to protect: before you write, count.
:::

## 2.3 The prediction routine — what to do in the silences

You are given short pauses before each section to read the questions. This time is not a courtesy; it is the most valuable thirty seconds in the test. Use it for a fixed four-step routine.

:::key
**The four-step pre-listening routine**
**1. Read the instruction and circle the word limit.** Write "2W" or "3W+N" at the top of the section.
**2. Underline the keywords** in each question — the content words that carry the meaning.
**3. Predict the grammar of the gap.** Is a noun needed? Singular or plural? A number? A date? A name? A verb? Write a tiny note: `n.pl`, `£`, `day`, `time`.
**4. Predict the paraphrase.** The recording will not use the words on your question paper. If the question says "the cost of membership", expect to hear *how much it is to join*, *the fee*, *you'd pay*.
:::

Step 3 is the one most candidates skip, and it is the one that catches errors. Consider:

> *Students must bring their own ____________ to the first session.*

The gap is after a possessive determiner and before a prepositional phrase. It must be a **noun**, and because "their own" suggests one item each, it is plausibly **singular or plural** — so listen for the -s. If you hear "you'll want to bring notebooks", the answer is `notebooks`. If you write `notebook`, you lose the mark. The grammar of the sentence is a free hint that arrives before the audio does.

### Predicting from the number type

| Gap context | Listen for |
| --- | --- |
| "costs £____" | A price; watch for corrections: "it's £40 — sorry, £45 for non-members" |
| "on ____" | A day or date: "the fourteenth of March" &rarr; `14 March` |
| "at ____" | A time: "half past nine" &rarr; `9.30` |
| "call ____" | A phone number, read in groups; "double four" = `44`; "oh" = `0` |
| "number ____" | Room, platform, house number |
| "____ per cent" | A percentage |

## 2.4 Signposting: how speakers tell you the answer is coming

Native speakers mark important information with predictable verbal packaging. Learning to hear the packaging is learning to hear the answer arriving.

| Function | Signals |
| --- | --- |
| **Emphasis / importance** | *the key thing is*, *what really matters*, *above all*, *don't forget*, *make sure you* |
| **Listing / sequence** | *firstly*, *then*, *after that*, *finally*, *another thing* |
| **Contrast / change of direction** | *however*, *although*, *on the other hand*, *having said that*, *mind you* |
| **Correction (the distractor trap)** | *actually*, *sorry*, *no wait*, *I mean*, *let me rephrase*, *in fact* |
| **Exemplification** | *for instance*, *such as*, *like*, *namely* |
| **Conclusion / summary** | *so overall*, *to sum up*, *the point is* |

The correction signals deserve their own section, because they are how the test separates Band 6 from Band 8.

## 2.5 Distractors — the single biggest source of lost marks

A distractor is a plausible wrong answer that is spoken *before* the right one. The test designers know that candidates write the first thing that fits and stop listening.

:::warn
**The three distractor patterns**
**1. Self-correction.** "The meeting's on Tuesday — sorry, no, Wednesday, they moved it."
Answer: **Wednesday**.
**2. Rejected option.** "We could use the main hall, but it's being redecorated, so we'll be in the Library Room."
Answer: **Library Room**.
**3. Negation.** "You don't need to bring a laptop; the tablets are provided."
Answer: **tablets** (and NOT `laptop`).
In every case, the wrong answer is spoken first, clearly, and matches the question's keywords. Never write and relax. Keep listening until the speaker has moved on to the next topic.
:::

A practical technique: **write in pencil, lightly, and be ready to cross out.** Candidates who commit in ink, psychologically, stop listening. Treat your first answer as a hypothesis that the next eight seconds may overturn.

## 2.6 The ten question types

### Type 1 — Form / Note / Table / Flow-chart completion

The most common Part 1 and Part 4 type. You are completing a structured document.

**Strategy.** The layout tells you the answer type. In a form, "Surname: ____" means a name that will be spelled out. "Address: ____" means a street, and numbers come first. In a flow-chart, arrows mean stages of a process, so answers are often verbs or noun phrases in a sequence.

:::key
**Spelling is marked.** A correctly heard answer spelled wrongly scores zero. Names and places are almost always spelled out letter by letter in the recording precisely so that spelling can be tested. Write the letters as you hear them, then check.
**The confusable letter pairs in spoken English:** B/P/V, D/T, M/N, S/F, G/J, I/E, A/E/8. When a speaker spells, they often clarify: "S for sugar", "double L", "that's with a K not a C".
:::

**Practice 2.1 — Form completion.** *Write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER.*

> **LIBRARY MEMBERSHIP FORM**
> Name: Katherine **(1)** ____________
> Address: **(2)** ____________ Road, Millbrook
> Occupation: **(3)** ____________
> Membership type: **(4)** ____________
> Annual fee: £ **(5)** ____________
> Card ready on: **(6)** ____________

*Transcript extract:* "Right, can I take your name? — Katherine Villiers. That's V-I-double L-I-E-R-S. — Lovely. And the address? — It's 37 Bracken Road, that's B-R-A-C-K-E-N, in Millbrook. — Thank you. And your occupation? — I'm a radiographer at the hospital. — Now, we do three types: standard, concession and family. Students and over-65s get the concession rate. — I'd like the family one, my two children read constantly. — Family it is. That's normally £48 a year, but there's an offer on at the moment so it's £36. — And when can I collect the card? — Normally five working days, but as you've applied in person it'll be ready on Thursday."

**Key:** (1) `Villiers` (2) `37 Bracken` (3) `radiographer` (4) `family` (5) `36` — *not 48; the offer overrides* (6) `Thursday` — *not "five working days"*.

Notice questions 5 and 6: both are distractor traps of the "rejected option" kind. The wrong number and the wrong timeframe were both spoken first.

### Type 2 — Sentence completion

You complete a sentence with words **from the recording**, within the word limit.

**Strategy.** Read the whole sentence, not just the words next to the gap. The sentence is a paraphrase of what will be said; the gap is the one part that isn't paraphrased. Check that your answer makes the sentence grammatically correct — if it doesn't, it is wrong.

### Type 3 — Short-answer questions

"Answer the questions below. Write NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS."

**Strategy.** The question word tells you the answer type: *What* &rarr; thing; *Where* &rarr; place; *When* &rarr; time; *Who* &rarr; person; *How many* &rarr; number; *Why* &rarr; reason (listen for *because*, *since*, *so that*, *the reason is*).

Watch for **plural traps**: "What TWO things must students bring?" requires two items, and both must be right for the mark.

### Type 4 — Multiple choice

Three options (A/B/C), occasionally longer lists where you choose two or three from five.

**Strategy.** This type punishes keyword-matching more than any other. All three options are usually *mentioned* in the recording; only one is *true in the way the question asks*.

:::warn
**How the three options are designed**
**The right answer** is paraphrased — it never uses the recording's exact words.
**Distractor 1** uses the recording's exact words but reverses, exaggerates or mistimes the meaning.
**Distractor 2** is true but answers a different question, or is true of a different speaker.
So: the option that sounds most familiar is the one most likely to be wrong.
:::

*Example.*
> **Q: The speaker says the new timetable is**
> A) more popular with students than the old one
> B) difficult for part-time staff
> C) going to be reviewed next term

*Recording:* "Students seem to like it — certainly we've had fewer complaints. The real issue has been our part-time colleagues, who've found the early starts almost impossible to accommodate. We'll live with it for now and see."

A is *nearly* true ("seem to like it", "fewer complaints") but is not what "the speaker says" most strongly; C is vague ("see" is not "review next term"); **B** is the answer, paraphrased from "almost impossible to accommodate".

Where the question says **"Choose TWO letters"**, the two correct options are usually separated in the recording by a stretch of irrelevant speech. Do not expect them consecutively.

### Type 5 — Matching

You match a list of items (people, places, years) to a set of options (A&ndash;F).

**Strategy.** The options are static; the items arrive in recording order. So track the *items*, not the options. Write the option letter next to the item as it is mentioned. Where options can be used more than once, the instruction says so explicitly — read it.

A very common Part 3 variant matches **opinions to speakers**: "Who thinks X — the tutor, James or Sarah?" Here the key skill is voice discrimination and attribution. Listen for *I think*, *in my view*, *I'd say*, and the agreement/disagreement markers: *absolutely*, *I'm not so sure*, *that's true, but*, *exactly*.

### Type 6 — Plan, map and diagram labelling

The type students fear most, and the course drills it heavily. You label locations on a map or parts of a diagram.

:::key
**The map routine**
**1. Find yourself.** Locate the entrance, the "you are here" marker, or the starting point named in the recording. Put your pencil on it.
**2. Establish orientation.** Note which way is north; identify the main axis (a road, a corridor, a river).
**3. Read the labels already given.** These are the landmarks the speaker will navigate by.
**4. Move your pencil.** As the speaker describes the route, physically trace it. Do not try to hold the route in your head.
:::

**The language of spatial description — memorise this.**

| Direction | Position | Sequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| go straight ahead / carry on | opposite / facing | first you come to |
| turn left / right | next to / beside / adjacent to | then / after that |
| take the first turning on your left | between X and Y | just before you reach |
| head north / south | in the corner of | immediately after |
| go past / go through | at the far end | at the very back |
| cross over | on your left-hand side | if you keep going |
| bear left (a gentle turn) | behind / in front of | you'll eventually come to |
| go up / down (stairs, a slope) | halfway along | as soon as you enter |
| all the way round | in the middle of | finally |

**Practice 2.2 — Map labelling.** A visitor centre. Entrance at the bottom (south). A central corridor runs north. Rooms A&ndash;H line both sides.

*Transcript extract:* "So you come in through the main doors here at the south end. Immediately on your left as you enter is the cloakroom — do leave your bags there. Directly opposite it, on your right, is the ticket desk. Now walk straight up the corridor. About halfway along on the right-hand side you'll find the cafe, which is open until four. Just past the cafe, still on the right, is the gift shop. On the left-hand side, opposite the gift shop, we have the main exhibition hall. And right at the far end of the corridor, straight ahead of you, is the lecture theatre."

If the map's rooms are lettered clockwise from the entrance, you can now place: cloakroom, ticket desk, cafe, gift shop, exhibition hall, lecture theatre — six answers from one paragraph, entirely through spatial prepositions. Note how often "opposite" and "just past" carry the entire mark.

### Type 7 — Summary completion

A paragraph-length summary of part of the recording, with gaps. Sometimes with a word box, sometimes without.

**Strategy.** If there is a **word box**, the words in it will *not* be the words in the recording — they are synonyms. Do the vocabulary work first: group the box words by part of speech, then by meaning. If there is **no box**, the words come from the recording verbatim and the word limit applies.

### Type 8 — Table completion

Common in Part 4. Rows and columns give you the structure of the lecture.

**Strategy.** The column headings tell you the answer category. Read across *and* down — if three cells in a column contain dates, the fourth is a date.

### Type 9 — Flow-chart completion

A process with stages linked by arrows.

**Strategy.** The answer is usually a noun or a verb in a fixed grammatical slot. Listen for sequencers: *first*, *next*, *once that's done*, *the following stage*, *finally*.

### Type 10 — Classification

Items are assigned to categories (e.g. "Which period does each building belong to: A 1800s, B 1900s, C 2000s?").

**Strategy.** Only two or three categories, many items. The categories repeat, so the recording will cycle through them. Track items in order and write letters.

## 2.7 Numbers, letters, dates: the mechanical marks

These questions are free marks that candidates throw away through unfamiliarity with spoken conventions.

| Spoken | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "double four" | 44 |
| "treble eight" / "triple eight" | 888 |
| "oh seven nine" (phone) | 079 |
| "nineteen ninety-four" | 1994 |
| "two thousand and nine" | 2009 |
| "twenty twenty-three" | 2023 |
| "the third of June" / "June the third" | 3 June (or 3rd June) |
| "a quarter past eight" | 8.15 |
| "twenty to seven" | 6.40 |
| "half past nine" | 9.30 |
| "half nine" (informal British) | 9.30 |
| "fifteen hundred hours" | 15.00 / 3 pm |
| "a hundred and twenty thousand" | 120,000 |
| "one point five million" | 1.5 million / 1,500,000 |
| "thirty pounds fifty" | £30.50 |
| "forty per cent" | 40% |

:::warn
**Thirteen or thirty?**
The distinction is **stress**, not vowel. *THIR-teen* has a strong first syllable and a clearly pronounced final /n/. *THIR-ty* ends in a flapped or unreleased /t/ and a short /i/. In connected speech, listen for the following word: "thirteen POUNDS" keeps stress on *-teen* when final. If genuinely unsure, use context — a course fee is more likely £30 than £13; a class size is more likely 13 than 30.
:::

## 2.9 Part-specific tactics

### Part 1 — bank the marks

Part 1 is the only section where near-perfection is realistic. It is a transaction, so the information is concrete: names, numbers, addresses, times, prices. Target 10/10. Every mark dropped here must be clawed back in Part 4, where it is four times harder.

### Part 2 — the map or the matching

Expect a monologue with a clear spatial or organisational structure. The speaker is usually giving a tour or introducing a facility. Follow the *physical* or *chronological* order.

### Part 3 — the opinion minefield

Two to four speakers, often a tutor and two students, discussing a project. The difficulty is attribution and the density of hedged opinion. Key phrases to track:

| Agreeing | Disagreeing | Partially agreeing | Hedging |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| absolutely | I'm not convinced | up to a point | it tends to |
| that's exactly it | not necessarily | I see what you mean, but | it seems that |
| precisely | I'd question that | that's fair, although | arguably |
| couldn't agree more | on the contrary | yes and no | more or less |
| good point | that's not quite right | broadly, yes | to some extent |

### Part 4 — the academic lecture

Ten questions, one speaker, no break in the middle. The single biggest risk is drifting.

**Tactics.** Read all ten questions in the preparation time (you get one longer pause for the whole part). Because it is a structured lecture, the speaker will signpost: "I want to look at three factors... the first of these is... turning now to... but perhaps the most significant..." Use those signposts as position markers. If you lose the thread, jump forward two questions and wait for the signpost that matches.

## 2.10 Transfer, checking and the ten minutes

**Paper-based:** ten minutes at the end. Use them like this — five minutes transferring carefully, three minutes checking spelling and plurals, two minutes filling every blank with a guess. **Never leave a blank.** There is no negative marking.

**Computer-based:** no transfer time, two minutes to review. Type your answers directly and use the review flag.

:::key
**The four-point final check**
1. **Word limit** — count the words in every completion answer.
2. **Plurals** — does the sentence need -s? Did you hear -s?
3. **Spelling** — especially of any word that was spelled out.
4. **Blanks** — none left. Guess something plausible and of the right word class.
:::

## 2.11 A four-week Listening training programme

**Week 1 — Accuracy.** Two sections a day. Do not aim for speed; aim for zero avoidable errors. After every test, categorise your mistakes using the four labels from Chapter 1.5.

**Week 2 — Prediction.** Before each section, spend a full minute writing your predictions for every gap: word class, number/name/noun, likely paraphrase. Then listen. Score your predictions as well as your answers.

**Week 3 — Dictation.** Take a two-minute stretch of Part 3 or Part 4 audio and transcribe it completely, word for word, replaying as needed. This is slow and unpleasant and it is the single most effective listening exercise in existence. It forces you to hear the function words, the contractions and the weak forms that carry grammar.

**Week 4 — Full tests under pressure.** One complete test a day, timed, no pauses, plus the transfer. Review only your errors.

### Passive listening that actually helps

Background English does very little on its own. Make it active:
- **Podcasts with transcripts.** Listen once without, once with, once without again.
- **Lectures on unfamiliar subjects.** Part 4 topics are deliberately foreign to you. Practise following structure when you don't know the content.
- **Multiple accents.** IELTS uses British, Australian, New Zealand, Canadian and American speakers. If you only train on American English, the Australian Part 2 will hurt.

## 2.12 Listening diagnostic checklist

Tick honestly after every practice test.

- I read the word limit and wrote it at the top of each section.
- I underlined keywords in every question before the audio began.
- I predicted the word class of every gap.
- When I missed an answer, I moved on within two seconds.
- I did not write my first plausible answer and stop listening.
- I checked every completion answer against the word limit.
- I left no blanks.
- I spelled every proper noun exactly as it was spelled out.
- I wrote numbers as digits.
- I checked every plural.
