## 8.2 Passage 1 — The Rise of Repair Cafés (13 questions)

**A.** In 2009, Martine Postma, a Dutch journalist concerned about waste, organised the first Repair Café in Amsterdam: a free meeting place where volunteers fix broken household items while their owners watch and learn. Seventeen years later, more than 2,500 Repair Cafés operate in 40 countries, from Tokyo to Toronto. The concept is disarmingly simple — people bring faulty toasters, torn jackets, or wobbling chairs, and leave with working possessions and new skills — yet researchers say it challenges the deepest habits of consumer culture.

**B.** The environmental argument is powerful. The average European generates 16 kilograms of electronic waste per year, much of it from appliances discarded for minor, fixable faults. A 2023 Delft University study estimated that every Repair Café session — typically 25 repairs in three hours — diverts around 50 kilograms of goods from landfill and saves roughly 500 kilograms of carbon dioxide emissions when manufacturing replacements is taken into account. "Repair is the forgotten twin of recycling," says Dr Lena Voss, the study's lead author. "Recycling breaks things down; repair keeps their value alive."

**C.** Just as important, supporters argue, is the social effect. Unlike conventional repair shops, cafés require owners to participate in the fix, turning a commercial transaction into a shared task. Retired engineers work alongside teenagers; newcomers to a city find conversation over a soldering iron. Surveys consistently show that visitors rate the community atmosphere as highly as the repairs themselves. Some municipalities now fund cafés explicitly as anti-loneliness projects rather than environmental ones.

**D.** Critics, however, question whether the movement can scale. Manufacturers, they note, continue to design products that resist repair — glued batteries, proprietary screws, spare parts priced above replacement cost. "A volunteer with a screwdriver cannot defeat an industry that profits from disposability," argues consumer analyst Raj Mehta. Campaigners respond by linking cafés to the broader "right to repair" movement, which has already secured legislation in the EU, the UK, and several US states requiring manufacturers to supply parts and manuals for up to ten years.

**E.** What is undisputed is the movement's educational power. Regular visitors report growing confidence: 70% say they now attempt simple repairs at home, and schools in Belgium and Germany have added café-style workshops to their curricula. Postma herself sees this as the true metric of success. "Every person who opens a device instead of binning it has changed their relationship with material things," she says. "That change, multiplied by millions, is what will save resources — not any single repaired kettle."

## 8.3 Passage 2 — Sleep and Memory (14 questions)

**A.** For decades, sleep was regarded as a passive state — the brain simply "switching off" until morning. Modern neuroscience has overturned that picture entirely. Using high-density EEG and brain imaging, researchers have shown that the sleeping brain is intensely active, replaying the day's experiences in fast-forward and deciding, somehow, which to keep. "Sleep is not the absence of waking," says Professor Daniel Okafor of the University of Edinburgh. "It is a different kind of work."

**B.** The key process is memory consolidation: the transfer of fragile new memories from the hippocampus, the brain's short-term hub, to the neocortex for long-term storage. This transfer happens mainly during deep slow-wave sleep, in the first half of the night, when bursts of neural activity synchronise the two regions. In a landmark 2022 experiment, Okafor's team taught volunteers sequences of finger movements, then monitored their sleep. Those who obtained at least 90 minutes of deep sleep performed the sequences 24% faster the next morning — without any further practice. The improvement, the researchers showed, correlated precisely with the number of synchronised bursts.

**C.** A second sleep stage, REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, appears to handle emotional memories differently. During REM, the brain replays upsetting experiences while stress chemistry is suppressed, effectively stripping the emotional charge from the memory while preserving its content. This may explain the familiar observation that distressing events feel more manageable "after a good night's sleep" — and why trauma sufferers, whose sleep is fragmented, struggle to recover. Clinical trials are now testing whether strengthening REM sleep can accelerate recovery from post-traumatic stress.

**D.** The practical implications extend to the classroom. A 2024 study of 8,000 secondary-school students found that those sleeping fewer than seven hours achieved, on average, half a grade less per subject than those sleeping eight or more — even after controlling for study time, ability, and background. Crucially, the deficit came not from drowsiness but from impaired consolidation: the short sleepers had studied as long yet retained less. Several schools have responded by delaying start times, with early results showing measurable gains in retention-heavy subjects such as languages and science.

**E.** Yet misconceptions persist. Many students still believe in all-night revision sessions before exams — a strategy the evidence comprehensively condemns. "Pulling an all-nighter is like writing an essay and then deleting the file," Okafor says. Equally misguided is reliance on weekend catch-up sleep, which repays drowsiness but cannot retroactively consolidate memories the brain has already discarded. The prescription, researchers agree, is unglamorous: regular hours, seven to nine of them, protected as fiercely as study time itself.

## 8.4 Passage 3 — The Economics of Attention (13 questions)

The modern economy runs on a resource no one can manufacture: human attention. Every day, the average smartphone user checks their device 144 times and spends over four hours staring at its screen. Advertisers now pay more than £500 billion annually for slices of that gaze. "Attention has become the scarcest commodity of the digital age," writes economist Priya Raman. "Land, labour, and capital have been joined — some would say eclipsed — by a fourth factor of production." Understanding how this market works, and who it serves, has become one of the most urgent questions in economics.

The business model is straightforward. Platforms offer free services — search, social networks, video — and sell predictions about user behaviour to advertisers. The more precisely a platform can predict what you will click, the more its predictions are worth; and predictions improve with data, which requires engagement. This creates what researchers call the "engagement imperative": every design choice, from infinite scroll to autoplay, is optimised to maximise time-on-device. Tristan Harris, a former Google ethicist, puts it bluntly: "There are a thousand engineers on the other side of the screen whose job is to keep you there."

Defenders of the model argue that consumers are willing participants. Nobody is forced to use social media; the services are genuinely useful; and advertising funds free access for billions who could not otherwise afford it. Moreover, they claim, competition disciplines excesses — platforms that abuse attention lose users to rivals. The economist Tyler Cowen has argued that revealed preference settles the matter: "People spend four hours a day on their phones because, all things considered, they prefer it to the alternatives."

Critics respond that the market is rigged in three ways. First, information asymmetry: users cannot see how their behaviour is tracked, modelled, and manipulated, while platforms know almost everything. Second, behavioural exploitation: design leverages cognitive biases — variable rewards, social approval cues, loss aversion — that bypass rational choice, affecting children and vulnerable users most. Third, collective-action failure: each individual's decision to stay connected is reasonable, yet the aggregate outcome — a society of distracted citizens — is one few would choose. "It is like traffic," Raman writes. "Every driver is rational; the jam is insane."

What, then, should be done? Proposals cluster into three camps. The transparency camp demands that platforms disclose what they collect and how feeds are curated, trusting informed users to adjust. The competition camp seeks to break platform monopolies or mandate interoperability, betting that genuine choice would reward less extractive designs. The prohibition camp, the most radical, would ban specific mechanisms — infinite scroll, autoplay, behavioural advertising to minors — just as societies once banned other profitable but harmful practices. Each approach has prominent backers and plausible pilots, from the EU's Digital Services Act to South Korea's gaming curfews.

Beneath the policy debate lies a deeper philosophical question: what is attention for? The psychologist William James called the ability to control attention "the very root of judgment, character, and will." If he was right, then an economy built on capturing attention is not merely selling adverts — it is quietly reshaping the capacity for self-direction itself. Whether that price is worth the convenience is a calculation no market can make for us.

## 8.6 Passage 4 — The Right to Repair (10 questions)

Legislation is catching up with the screwdriver. In 2024, the European Union's new right-to-repair directive required manufacturers of phones, tablets, and appliances to supply spare parts for ten years and to pay for repairs within the legal guarantee period. France went further: its repairability index, displayed on products at the point of sale, scores devices from 1 to 10 — and early data suggest consumers genuinely use it, with high-scoring models gaining market share.

Manufacturers warn of unintended consequences. Design constraints, they argue, will slow innovation and raise prices; a glued, waterproof phone is thinner and more durable than a screwed, repairable one. "Consumers say they want repairability," one industry spokesperson said, "but they buy thinness." There is some evidence for this: despite the index, France's thinnest phones still outsell its most repairable ones.

Advocates counter that the trade-off is exaggerated. They point to Fairphone, a Dutch company whose modular handsets score 10/10 yet remain slim enough for mainstream buyers, and to a new generation of repair start-ups offering 30-minute screen fixes in shopping centres. More fundamentally, they argue, the market cannot price what it cannot see: the cobalt mined by children, the e-waste dumped in Ghana, the carbon of manufacturing a phone that lasts two years. Regulation, in this view, corrects a market failure rather than distorting a market success.

The writer's own view is that both sides understate the cultural shift already underway. Repair cafés, refurbished-phone retailers, and "fix-it" influencers with millions of followers suggest that durability is becoming, however slowly, a status symbol. Legislation helps — but as with seatbelts and smoking bans, the law works best when it confirms a change the public has already begun to want.
