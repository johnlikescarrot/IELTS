# Chapter 7 — Speaking
^ Eleven to fourteen minutes, three parts, four criteria: a conversation that is also an examination

The course devotes three full sessions to Speaking, and the teacher's framing is worth keeping in mind throughout: Speaking and Writing are the **productive** skills. "You are going to produce language, and as long as you produce language, you are going to be judged." Listening and Reading ask you to recognise; Speaking asks you to generate, in real time, under observation.

The good news is that the format is completely predictable, the topics are drawn from a finite set, and the four criteria are published.

## 7.1 The format

| Part | Time | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Introduction & ID check** | ~1 min | Name, ID verification. Not assessed. |
| **Part 1 — Interview** | 4&ndash;5 min | Questions on 2&ndash;3 familiar topics: home, work, study, hobbies, food, weather, travel |
| **Part 2 — Long turn** | 3&ndash;4 min | A cue card. 1 minute preparation, 1&ndash;2 minutes speaking, then 1&ndash;2 rounding-off questions |
| **Part 3 — Discussion** | 4&ndash;5 min | Abstract questions thematically linked to the Part 2 topic |

The test is recorded. It is conducted face to face (or by video call). The examiner is following a script and cannot deviate much — which means the questions are highly predictable.

## 7.2 The four criteria, and what they actually reward

Each criterion is worth **25%**.

### Fluency and Coherence

Not speed. **Fluency** is the ability to keep going without unnatural pausing, excessive self-correction or constant hesitation. **Coherence** is whether your ideas connect logically and are marked with appropriate discourse signals.

| Band | Description |
| --- | --- |
| 5 | Usually maintains flow but with repetition, self-correction and slow speech; over-uses simple connectives |
| 6 | Willing to speak at length, though coherence may be lost through hesitation or repetition; uses a range of connectives, sometimes inaccurately |
| 7 | Speaks at length without noticeable effort; some hesitation is language-related but occasional; uses a range of connectives flexibly |
| 8 | Fluent with only occasional repetition or self-correction; hesitation is content-related, not language-related; develops topics coherently |

:::key
**The critical distinction at Band 7+: hesitation is content-related, not language-related.**
Pausing to think about *what* to say is normal and human, and is not penalised. Pausing because you are hunting for a word is penalised. This is why fillers matter — they let you buy thinking time without silence.
:::

### Lexical Resource

Range, precision, appropriacy, collocation, and the ability to **paraphrase** when the exact word is missing.

### Grammatical Range and Accuracy

Variety of structures (not just accuracy) and how often errors occur. A candidate who speaks only in simple sentences with no errors cannot reach Band 7; range is half the criterion.

### Pronunciation

Intelligibility, individual sounds, word stress, sentence stress, intonation, connected speech. Chapter 11 treats this in full.

:::key
**Accent is not assessed.**
The teacher is direct on this: knowing both American and British accents is useful background knowledge, "but it is not the important point." You are not required to sound British, American or Australian. You are required to be **intelligible**, to place stress correctly, and to use intonation that supports meaning. A strong regional accent that is clearly understood scores well; a "neutral" accent with misplaced word stress does not.
:::

## 7.3 Part 1 — the interview

Four to five minutes, three topics, roughly four questions each. The examiner is warming you up and sampling your everyday language.

:::warn
**The single most damaging Part 1 habit: the short answer.**
> *Examiner: Do you live in a flat or a house?*
> *Candidate: I live in a house.*
The teacher's response to exactly this in class: "Is that a good answer? In a speaking test, never, never give a short answer — never. You need to support your answer with details or reasons."
A four-word reply to a five-second question wastes 8% of your Part 1 time and demonstrates nothing. You have given the examiner no language to assess.
:::

### The 2&ndash;3 sentence rule

Every Part 1 answer should last **20&ndash;40 seconds** and contain:

1. **The direct answer.**
2. **A reason or detail.**
3. **An extension** — an example, a contrast, a personal note, or a qualification.

**Rebuilt:**

> "I live in a house, actually — it's on the outskirts of the city, about twenty minutes from the centre. I like it a lot because it's quite modern and cosy, and there's a small garden at the back where I spend most of my free time reading when the weather allows. I did live in a flat while I was studying, but I found it a bit cramped after a while."

Three sentences. A direct answer, two reasons, a contrast with a past situation, a relative clause, a *when*-clause and a *while*-clause. That is roughly thirty-five seconds and it is Band 7+ material — from an entirely ordinary question.

:::warn
**But do not over-extend either.** A ninety-second answer to "Do you like coffee?" is a different error: the examiner has a script to get through and will interrupt you, which breaks your rhythm. Twenty to forty seconds. Then stop and let them ask the next one.
:::

### Extension techniques

| Technique | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Give a reason** | "...mainly because it's so much quieter than where I grew up." |
| **Give an example** | "...for instance, last weekend I spent the whole Sunday out there." |
| **Contrast with the past** | "...though I used to hate it when I was younger." |
| **Contrast with others** | "...unlike most of my friends, who'd much rather be in the city centre." |
| **Add a condition** | "...at least when the weather's good; in winter it's a different story." |
| **Add a feeling** | "...which I find genuinely relaxing after a long day." |
| **Qualify** | "...well, most of the time, anyway." |
| **Speculate** | "...I imagine I'll feel differently once I have children." |

Learn two or three of these so thoroughly that they are automatic. They turn any four-word answer into a thirty-second one.

### Fillers: the legitimate ones

The course recommends fillers explicitly as a way to "pave the way" for your answer and buy thinking time. Used well they *improve* fluency scores because they replace silence with natural speech.

| Function | Fillers |
| --- | --- |
| Buying time | *Well...*, *Let me think...*, *That's an interesting question...*, *Actually...* |
| Honesty marker | *To be honest...*, *Frankly...*, *I must admit...* |
| Softening | *I suppose...*, *I'd say...*, *Sort of...*, *Kind of...* |
| Generalising | *Generally speaking...*, *On the whole...*, *More often than not...* |
| Correcting yourself | *Or rather...*, *What I mean is...*, *Let me put it another way...* |

:::warn
**The fillers that hurt you**
**"Um", "uh", "er"** — non-lexical. In quantity they read as hesitation, not as discourse management.
**Repeating the question** — "Do I like cooking? Well, do I like cooking..." Once is a stalling tactic; twice is a pattern.
**"Yanni", "eh", and other first-language fillers.** The teacher raises this directly with his class: a word from your own language dropped into English speech "out of context" is noticeable and marks you down. Even very common English words can misfire: he gives the example of a graduation speech in which "sorry, sorry" and "okay" kept appearing — familiar words, but wrong in context.
**"Like" as a verbal tic**, repeated every few words.
:::

### Part 1 topic bank

The topics are drawn from a short, stable list. Prepare two or three specific details for each so you are never hunting.

| Category | Topics |
| --- | --- |
| **You** | Work or study; where you live; your home; your family; your daily routine; your hometown |
| **Preferences** | Food and cooking; music; films; books; sport; shopping; colours; weather and seasons |
| **Activities** | Free time; exercise; travel; social media; photography; going out; celebrations |
| **Habits** | Sleep; transport; keeping in touch; the internet; learning languages; being on time |
| **Abstract-lite** | Friends; neighbours; memory; the news; the environment; technology at home |

**Sample Part 1 exchanges — Band 7+ answers.**

> *What do you do?*
> "I'm a community pharmacist — I work at a call centre for a pharmacy chain based in the business district. It's not the sort of pharmacy work most people picture; I spend the day advising patients over the phone rather than standing behind a counter, which I actually prefer."

> *What do you like about your job?*
> "The variety, mostly. I speak to people from all sorts of different backgrounds every day, and no two conversations are quite the same. It can be demanding when someone's anxious about their medication, but being able to reassure them is genuinely the best part of it."

> *Do you have a large family or a small one?*
> "It's fairly small, actually — it consists of my mother, my two brothers and me. We live in a village in the countryside, so we're surrounded by fields, which sounds idyllic and mostly is. We're all quite close, partly because there aren't many of us."

*Notice: "it consists of" — the teacher corrects a student on exactly this, from "it is consist of" to "it consists of". Small grammatical precision, repeatedly rewarded.*

## 7.4 Part 2 — the long turn

You are handed a card, given **one minute** to prepare with paper and pencil, and asked to speak for **one to two minutes**.

> **Describe a place you have visited that you would recommend to others.**
> *You should say:*
> *&bull; where it is*
> *&bull; when you went there*
> *&bull; what you did there*
> *and explain why you would recommend it to others.*

:::key
**The structure of the card is the structure of your answer.**
Three bullets plus a final "explain" prompt. That is four sections. Speak for roughly 20 seconds on each of the first three and 40&ndash;50 seconds on the last — the "explain" prompt always carries the most weight, because it requires justification rather than description.
:::

### Using the preparation minute

Do not write sentences. You cannot read them out convincingly and you will run out of time.

**Write six to eight keywords**, two per bullet, in a column. Then, if there is time, one connecting phrase. That's it.

> *Where:* Petra, southern Jordan — desert, canyon
> *When:* three years ago, April, with my brother
> *What:* walked the Siq at dawn, the Treasury, climbed to the Monastery
> *Why:* scale, silence, the reveal, unlike photos

Twenty-five words. Enough to prevent a blank mind; not enough to tempt you into reading.

### Speaking for two minutes

Two minutes is longer than it feels. The commonest failure is finishing in forty seconds and standing in silence.

**The techniques that fill time honestly:**

| Technique | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Set the scene** | "This was back in April three years ago, just after I'd finished my degree..." |
| **Sensory detail** | "The thing I remember most vividly is how cold it was at six in the morning, and how completely silent." |
| **Narrative** | Tell it as a story with a sequence: first, then, after that, eventually. |
| **Compare** | "I'd seen photographs, obviously, but none of them prepare you for the scale of it." |
| **Feelings then and now** | "At the time I was mostly exhausted; looking back, it's the trip I'd least want to undo." |
| **Speculate** | "If I went again, I'd stay longer and do the northern trail." |
| **Generalise outward** | "And I think that's why I'd recommend it to anyone, really — because..." |

:::warn
**Do not memorise a speech.** Examiners hear hundreds of these and recognise them instantly — the rhythm flattens, the vocabulary jumps a level above your Part 1 answers, and the moment you are asked a follow-up question the fluency collapses. A memorised answer that does not quite match the card is also penalised on Task Response grounds.
**Do adapt a prepared story.** Having three or four rich personal experiences you can bend to fit different cards is legitimate and sensible. A trip, a person who influenced you, a difficult decision, a skill you learned — these can be reshaped for a large fraction of cue cards.
:::

### Cue card categories

| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **A person** | Someone you admire; a friend; a teacher; a famous person; an elderly person you know; someone who helped you |
| **A place** | A city you visited; a quiet place; your ideal house; a park; a place you'd like to work |
| **An object** | A gift you received; something you bought recently; a piece of technology; a photograph |
| **An event** | A celebration; a journey; a time you were late; an achievement; a time you helped someone |
| **An activity** | A hobby; a sport; something you learned; something you do to relax |
| **An experience** | A difficult decision; a time you changed your mind; a time you waited for something |
| **Media** | A book, film, TV programme, song, website that had an impact on you |

### Model Part 2 answer (Band 8)

> **Describe a skill you learned that was difficult at first.**

"I'd like to talk about learning to swim, which I only did properly at the age of twenty-six, and which was far and away the most humiliating thing I've ever attempted.

It happened about four years ago. I'd moved to a coastal city for work and found myself in the slightly ridiculous position of living ten minutes from the sea and being genuinely frightened of it. So I signed up for adult beginners' lessons at the local pool, which took a certain amount of swallowing my pride, because the class was largely made up of people in their sixties who were considerably braver than I was.

What made it difficult wasn't the physical side, particularly. It was that I'd absorbed this idea, somewhere along the way, that I simply wasn't a person who could swim — and unlearning that took much longer than learning the actual strokes. For the first six or seven weeks I couldn't put my face in the water without panicking. My instructor was remarkably patient about it; she never once made me feel foolish, and she broke everything down into these absurdly small steps — first just breathing out underwater, then floating for three seconds, then four.

The breakthrough came quite suddenly, as I think these things often do. One evening I realised I'd swum the length of the pool without thinking about it, and after that the fear more or less evaporated.

I'd say it taught me something beyond swimming, honestly. The lesson was that the thing standing between me and a fairly ordinary skill was almost entirely a story I'd been telling myself, and that the only way through was to be patient and slightly embarrassed for a couple of months."

*Roughly two minutes. All four prompts covered. Past perfect, past continuous, relative clauses, a cleft sentence ("What made it difficult wasn't..."), conditional reflection, and a reflective conclusion. Idiomatic but not forced.*

### The rounding-off questions

After your long turn the examiner asks one or two short questions on the same topic. Answer briefly — a sentence or two. These are a transition, not a second long turn.

## 7.5 Part 3 — the discussion

Four to five minutes of abstract, analytical questions connected to the Part 2 theme. This is where Band 7 and Band 8 are separated, because the questions demand opinion, justification, comparison, speculation and evaluation — not personal description.

> *Part 2 was about a skill you learned. Part 3 might ask:*
> - Why do you think some adults are reluctant to learn new skills?
> - Is it better to learn practical skills or academic knowledge at school?
> - How has technology changed the way people acquire skills?
> - Do you think governments should fund adult education? Why?

:::key
**The Part 3 answer shape: Answer &rarr; Reason &rarr; Example &rarr; Balance**
**Answer:** state your position directly.
**Reason:** explain the mechanism.
**Example:** make it concrete.
**Balance:** acknowledge the other side or add a qualification.
Roughly 40&ndash;60 seconds per answer. Longer than Part 1, shorter than Part 2.
:::

**Worked example:**

> *Why are some adults reluctant to learn new skills?*

"I think the main barrier is psychological rather than practical. **[Answer]** Adults have usually built an identity around being competent at certain things, and learning something new means being visibly bad at it in front of other people, which is uncomfortable in a way that it simply isn't for a child. **[Reason]** You see this very clearly with languages — plenty of people will happily read a grammar book alone but won't speak a word in public, because the mistakes feel exposing. **[Example]** That said, I don't think it's universal; I'd argue it has a lot to do with the culture of the workplace or community someone's in. Where making mistakes is treated as normal, adults seem far more willing to start from scratch. **[Balance]**"

### Part 3 functional language

| Function | Expressions |
| --- | --- |
| **Giving opinion** | I'd argue that...; It seems to me that...; My own view is that...; I'm inclined to think... |
| **Speculating** | It may well be that...; I suspect...; Presumably...; It's conceivable that... |
| **Comparing** | Compared with...; Whereas in the past...; The difference is that... |
| **Generalising** | By and large...; As a rule...; Broadly speaking...; In most cases... |
| **Exemplifying** | Take X, for instance...; A good case in point would be... |
| **Conceding** | I take the point that...; There's certainly something in that, although...; Admittedly... |
| **Predicting** | I imagine we'll see...; The likelihood is that...; If current trends continue... |
| **Evaluating** | The crucial factor is...; What matters most is...; The real question is whether... |
| **Hedging** | It rather depends on...; To some extent...; Up to a point...; More often than not... |

**Hedging deserves emphasis.** Band 8 speakers rarely make absolute claims. "Governments should always fund adult education" is weaker English than "I'd say there's a strong case for public funding, at least where the skills in question have clear economic value." The hedge shows control of nuance and gives you grammatical structures to display.

### If you don't understand the question

This is allowed and does not cost you marks — provided you handle it in English.

- "Sorry, could you rephrase that?"
- "Do you mean [X] specifically, or [Y] more generally?"
- "I'm not sure I've understood — are you asking about...?"

In Part 3 (though not Part 1) you may ask for clarification freely. What you must not do is answer a different question because you did not understand.

### If you have no ideas

You are not assessed on the quality of your opinions. Any defensible position, defended, scores fully. Buy time honestly:

- "That's a difficult one. I suppose the first thing that comes to mind is..."
- "I haven't really thought about it before, but off the top of my head..."
- "I can see it from two angles, actually..."

## 7.6 Common Speaking failures

:::warn
**The nine habits that cap your Speaking band**
1. **Short answers** (Part 1). Covered above. The most common of all.
2. **Memorised speeches.** Detected instantly; penalised.
3. **Answering a different question** because you misheard and didn't ask.
4. **Repeating the question verbatim** to buy time.
5. **Speaking too fast** to seem fluent. Speed is not fluency; clarity is. Rushing produces mispronunciation and swallowed endings.
6. **Nervous silence.** The teacher raises this with a student who freezes: if you get nervous, you will not do well — so practise until the format itself is boring. Speak, even imperfectly; silence scores zero.
7. **Simple sentences only.** No subordination, no range. See Chapter 9.
8. **Repeating basic vocabulary** — *good, bad, nice, thing, very*. See Chapter 10.
9. **First-language fillers** and out-of-context discourse markers.
:::

### On nerves, specifically

The course addresses this directly, because several students visibly struggle with it in the recorded sessions. Three practical points:

1. **The examiner is not your adversary.** They are following a script and filling in a form. Their friendliness or coolness carries no information about your score.
2. **Preparation converts fear into boredom.** If you have done forty mock Part 2s, the forty-first is not frightening.
3. **Organise before speaking.** The teacher's advice: "Don't rush the answer. There should be some ideas arranged in your mind." A two-second pause before beginning is invisible; a twenty-second stumble halfway through is not.

## 7.7 Practising alone

You do not need a partner for most of this.

| Exercise | Method |
| --- | --- |
| **Record and review** | Answer five Part 1 questions, record, listen back with a notepad. Count: short answers, fillers, repeated words, grammatical errors. |
| **The two-minute drill** | Random cue card, one minute prep, speak for two minutes to a timer. Every day. Do not stop early. |
| **Extension drill** | Take ten one-word answers you'd naturally give and expand each to thirty seconds. |
| **Shadowing** | Play twenty seconds of a native speaker, then repeat it copying rhythm and intonation exactly. Best single exercise for pronunciation and fluency together. |
| **The opinion minute** | Pick a Part 3 question at random. Speak for sixty seconds using the Answer&ndash;Reason&ndash;Example&ndash;Balance shape. |
| **Vocabulary upgrading** | Listen to your own recording; every time you hear *good*, *bad*, *nice*, *thing* or *very*, write a better alternative and re-record the sentence. |

## 7.8 Speaking diagnostic checklist

- No Part 1 answer was shorter than two sentences.
- No Part 1 answer was longer than forty seconds.
- I filled the full two minutes in Part 2.
- I covered all four prompts on the cue card.
- I used at least three complex sentences per Part 3 answer.
- I used hedging language rather than absolute claims.
- I did not repeat the examiner's question back.
- I used English fillers, not first-language ones.
- I asked for clarification in English when I needed it.
- I did not rush; my word endings were audible.
