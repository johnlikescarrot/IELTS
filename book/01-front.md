# How This Book Was Made
^ Provenance, method, and the best way to use the twelve chapters that follow

This book has one unusual property: it was not written from a syllabus. It was written from a **classroom**.

The source material is the complete *12 Hours Full Course for Beginners — Master-Course of the 4 Components*, a live IELTS preparation course delivered over a series of sessions and published as twelve video parts. Every one of those twelve parts was transcribed in full and then studied line by line: the teacher's explanations, the rules he repeats until the class can recite them, the corrections he makes to students' live answers, the exercises he sets, the model sentences he builds on the whiteboard, and — just as valuable — the mistakes the students actually make, which are the same mistakes candidates make in the real exam.

What you are holding is that twelve-hour course reorganised into a reference book, with every rule made explicit, every half-finished example completed, every scattered remark gathered into the chapter where it belongs, and a very large amount of additional scaffolding added: full band descriptor tables, expanded language banks, worked model answers, complete practice sets and answer keys.

## What the original course covered

The course is built around the structure the teacher announces in the first session and keeps returning to: the four components, in the order the test itself presents them.

| Session block | Component | Principal content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parts 1&ndash;2 | **Listening** | Instruction-reading, word limits, all ten question types, prediction, signposting, distractors, spelling and number traps, full practice sets |
| Parts 3&ndash;4 | **Reading** | Skimming and scanning, topic sentences, True/False/Not Given, matching headings, paraphrase-hunting, summary and diagram completion, timing strategy |
| Parts 8&ndash;9, 11&ndash;12 | **Writing Task 1** | Academic data description (line graphs, bar charts, pie charts, tables, processes, maps) and General Training letters; overview rules, word counts, figure use, comparison language |
| Parts 9&ndash;12 | **Writing Task 2** | Essay types, four/five-paragraph architecture, thesis statements, topic sentences, cohesion, sentence variety, concession structures |
| Parts 5&ndash;7 | **Speaking** | The three parts, the four criteria, fluency and fillers, extending answers, the cue card, abstract discussion, pronunciation |
| Throughout | **Language systems** | Grammar for range and accuracy, vocabulary upgrading, pronunciation, accent awareness |

## The teacher's core convictions

Several ideas recur so often in the twelve hours that they function as the spine of the whole course. They are worth stating at the outset, because everything else in this book is an elaboration of them.

:::key
**The five convictions of the course**
**1. Reading and Listening are won by technique; Writing and Speaking are won by production.** As the teacher puts it, many candidates get good scores in Listening and Reading, "but when it comes to writing and speaking, now we need to stop a little bit — because writing and speaking are the productive skills. You are going to produce language, and as long as you produce language, you are going to be judged."
**2. Instructions are part of the test.** Word limits ("NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER") are not advice. Breaking them scores zero even when the content is right.
**3. The examiner marks a rubric, not an impression.** Four named criteria, each worth 25%. You can reverse-engineer every one of them.
**4. Paraphrase is the central skill of the entire exam.** Listening answers are paraphrased in the recording; reading answers are paraphrased in the passage; writing introductions must paraphrase the prompt; speaking answers must not echo the question.
**5. Never give a short answer, and never over-write.** In Speaking, a one-clause reply is a wasted question. In Writing, 300 words is not a sign of cleverness; it is a sign of poor control.
:::

## How to use this book

Read Chapter 1 once, for the architecture of the test. After that, the book is designed to be used in three different ways.

**As a course.** Work through Chapters 2&ndash;8 in order over eight to ten weeks, one component at a time, doing every exercise. Chapter 12 gives you three dated study plans (four weeks, eight weeks, twelve weeks) built exactly on that sequence.

**As a clinic.** If one skill is holding your overall band down, go straight to its chapter. Each component chapter is self-contained and ends with a diagnostic checklist and a set of practice tasks with keys.

**As a reference.** Chapters 9 (Grammar), 10 (Vocabulary and Paraphrase) and 11 (Pronunciation) are language banks. Keep returning to them while you write and speak. The appendices in Chapter 13 collect every list in the book — linking words, trend verbs, functional language, letter phrases, cue-card frameworks — in one place for revision in the final week.

### Conventions used throughout

- Text in quotation marks with an indent is **the teacher's own phrasing** from the recorded sessions, lightly cleaned of false starts.
- Boxes with a blue bar are **key rules** — the non-negotiables.
- Boxes with a red bar are **warnings** — the band-destroying mistakes.
- Boxes with a green bar are **band-raisers** — the things that separate 6.5 from 7.5.
- Model answers are written at the band level stated above them. Where two versions appear, compare them sentence by sentence; the gap between them *is* the lesson.

:::warn
**A word about honesty in preparation**
The single most common reason a well-prepared candidate underperforms is that they practised under conditions kinder than the exam. Untimed reading, a second listen to the recording, a dictionary open while writing, a rehearsed speech for the cue card. Every practice task in this book states its time limit. Keep it. A Band 7 essay written in fifty minutes is a Band 6 essay in the exam.
:::
