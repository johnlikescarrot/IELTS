# Chapter 6 — Writing Task 2: The Essay
^ Forty minutes, 250 words, two thirds of the Writing mark: the highest-value forty minutes in IELTS

Task 2 carries **twice the weight** of Task 1. It is also the task where candidates most often plateau, because the failures are structural rather than linguistic: essays that do not answer the question, paragraphs without a controlling idea, positions that shift halfway through.

The course treats Task 2 as an engineering problem — a fixed number of paragraphs, each with a fixed function, each containing a fixed sequence of sentence types. That approach is reproduced and completed here.

## 6.1 The brief and the constraints

You are given a statement or question and asked to write a discursive response. **Forty minutes. At least 250 words.**

:::key
**The numbers**
**40 minutes:** 5 planning, 30 writing, 5 checking.
**250 words minimum**, penalised automatically below it.
**260&ndash;300 words is the target.** Long enough to develop; short enough to control.
**Four or five paragraphs.** The teacher is explicit and repeats it: writing more than five paragraphs reduces your score, and "if you write your piece of writing in one or two or even three paragraphs, you will not get more than Band 5."
:::

:::warn
**The prohibitions — identical to Task 1**
No headings. No subheadings. No bullet points. No numbered lists. No idioms. The teacher, writing on the whiteboard in bullets, points at his own notes: *"the way I'm writing now is called bullet points — never use something like that in your essay."*
Also: no contractions (*don't*, *isn't*, *can't*) — Task 2 is formal. No "etc." No rhetorical questions in the conclusion. No statistics you have invented ("73% of people believe...").
:::

## 6.2 The five question types

Almost every Task 2 prompt belongs to one of five families. Identifying the family determines the structure, and misidentifying it is the single most common cause of a low Task Response score.

### Type 1 — Opinion (Agree or Disagree)

> *"Some people believe that university education should be free for all students. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"*

**Signals:** *To what extent do you agree or disagree? / Do you agree or disagree? / What is your opinion?*

**Requirement:** a clear, consistent position, defended throughout.

:::key
**"To what extent" permits three legitimate answers:**
**Total agreement** — "I strongly agree." Both body paragraphs support.
**Total disagreement** — "I firmly disagree." Both body paragraphs oppose.
**Partial agreement** — "I agree with respect to X, but not with respect to Y." Body 1 concedes, Body 2 argues. This is often the most sophisticated response, *provided your final position is unmistakable.*
What is **not** permitted is a balanced survey with no position. That is a different question type and scores badly here.
:::

### Type 2 — Discussion (Discuss both views and give your own opinion)

> *"Some people think children should start school at the age of four, while others believe seven is more appropriate. Discuss both views and give your own opinion."*

**Requirement:** genuinely discuss both, *and* state your own view. Candidates routinely omit the opinion and lose a band.

**Structure:** Body 1 = view A with reasons. Body 2 = view B with reasons. Your own opinion in the introduction and the conclusion, or as a third short paragraph.

:::warn
**Do not give equal weight and then claim a view.** If you argue side A for 100 words and side B for 100 words and then announce you favour A, you have not demonstrated why. Give slightly more development to the side you support, or add a short third paragraph justifying your choice.
:::

### Type 3 — Problem and Solution (or Cause and Solution)

> *"Traffic congestion is becoming a serious problem in many major cities. What are the causes of this, and what measures could be taken to address it?"*

**Requirement:** answer both halves. Two causes and two solutions, or one of each fully developed.

**Structure:** Body 1 = causes/problems. Body 2 = solutions. Make the solutions *answer* the causes; an essay whose solutions are unrelated to its stated causes is incoherent.

### Type 4 — Advantages and Disadvantages

> *"More and more people are working from home. Do the advantages of this development outweigh the disadvantages?"*

**Two variants, and the difference matters:**

| Wording | What is required |
| --- | --- |
| *"Discuss the advantages and disadvantages."* | A balanced account. Opinion optional. |
| *"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"* | A **verdict**. You must state which side is greater and why. |

The second wording appears more often, and candidates answer it as if it were the first. Read carefully.

### Type 5 — Two-part question (Direct questions)

> *"Many people now buy goods online rather than in shops. Why is this happening? Is it a positive or a negative development?"*

**Requirement:** answer both questions explicitly. Body 1 = question 1. Body 2 = question 2.

### Reading the prompt properly

Every prompt has three parts. Separate them before planning.

| Part | Function | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Background statement** | Context. Not to be argued with. | "In many countries, the proportion of older people is steadily increasing." |
| **The task** | The actual question. | "Does this trend have more positive or negative effects?" |
| **The instruction** | Format requirements. | "Give reasons for your answer and include relevant examples from your own experience." |

:::warn
**The commonest off-task error: answering the background instead of the task.** If the background says older populations are increasing and the task asks about effects, an essay arguing about *whether* populations are ageing is off-task and capped around Band 5. Underline the task sentence. Write it at the top of your plan.
**The second commonest: the hidden second question.** "Why is this happening, and is it a positive development?" contains two tasks. Missing one costs a band on Task Response.
:::

## 6.3 The architecture

| Paragraph | Function | Sentences | Words |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Introduction** | Paraphrase + outline/thesis | 2&ndash;3 | 40&ndash;55 |
| **2. Body 1** | First main idea, fully developed | 4&ndash;6 | 90&ndash;110 |
| **3. Body 2** | Second main idea, fully developed | 4&ndash;6 | 90&ndash;110 |
| **4. Conclusion** | Restate position, summarise | 2&ndash;3 | 35&ndash;50 |

A fifth paragraph is permitted where the question genuinely has three components (e.g. a third short paragraph stating your own opinion in a discussion essay). Five is the ceiling.

:::key
**Two developed body paragraphs beat three thin ones.**
In 280 words you can develop two ideas properly or mention four inadequately. The band descriptors reward *development* — explanation, consequence, example — not the number of ideas. This is the most common structural upgrade from Band 6 to Band 7.
:::

## 6.4 The introduction, sentence by sentence

Two sentences, three at most.

**Sentence 1 — Paraphrase the prompt.** Restate the topic in your own words. Never copy.

**Sentence 2 — The thesis statement.** State your position and, ideally, signal your two main ideas.

### The thesis statement

The teacher promises to "explain that in detail" and treats it as the essential element of the introduction. Here is the full account.

A thesis statement does three things: it **states your position**, it **previews your reasons**, and it **controls the whole essay** — every body paragraph must serve it.

| Question type | Thesis frame |
| --- | --- |
| Opinion | *In my view, [position], principally because [reason 1] and [reason 2].* |
| Opinion (partial) | *While I accept that [concession], I would argue that on balance [position].* |
| Discussion | *Although both positions have merit, I believe that [your side] is the more persuasive, for reasons of [A] and [B].* |
| Problem/solution | *This essay will argue that the phenomenon stems chiefly from [cause], and that the most effective response lies in [solution].* |
| Advantages/disadvantages | *Although [drawback] is a genuine concern, I would contend that the benefits — notably [A] and [B] — clearly outweigh it.* |
| Two-part | *This essay will suggest that the trend arises from [cause], and will argue that its overall impact is [positive/negative].* |

:::warn
**Avoid the mechanical announcement.** "In this essay I will discuss both sides and give my opinion" tells the examiner nothing, uses fifteen words, and appears in thousands of scripts. Replace it with a thesis that has actual content.
**Also avoid the false-fact opener.** "Since the dawn of time, education has been the most debated topic in the world." It is untrue, it is unoriginal, and it wastes twenty words. Start with the paraphrase.
:::

**Worked introduction.**

> **Prompt:** "Some people believe that the internet has brought people closer together, while others argue it has made us more isolated. Discuss both views and give your own opinion."

> "The question of whether digital connectivity has strengthened or eroded human relationships continues to divide opinion. While it is undeniable that online communication can substitute for genuine contact, I would argue that on balance the internet has expanded rather than diminished our social worlds, particularly for those whose circumstances limit face-to-face interaction."

*Forty-eight words. Topic paraphrased with no copied content words. Both views acknowledged. A clear position with a reason embedded. The essay now has a direction.*

## 6.5 Body paragraphs: the PEEL engine

Each body paragraph develops **one** idea. The course's emphasis on the **topic sentence** is the key: a paragraph without one is a list of sentences, not an argument.

:::key
**PEEL — the four moves of a body paragraph**
**P — Point (topic sentence).** The controlling idea, stated in one sentence, at the start.
**E — Explain.** Why is this true? What is the mechanism? Two sentences.
**E — Example / Evidence.** A concrete instance. One or two sentences.
**L — Link.** Connect back to the thesis, or forward to the next paragraph. One sentence.
:::

### The topic sentence

The single most valuable sentence in the essay after the thesis. It must:
- express **one** idea, not two;
- be a **claim**, not a fact or a question;
- **relate explicitly to the thesis**;
- appear **first** in the paragraph.

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| "Another thing is transport." | "The most immediate benefit of investment in public transport is a measurable reduction in urban congestion." |
| "There are many advantages of working from home, and also disadvantages." | "Remote working delivers its clearest gains in employee autonomy." |
| "Why do people move to cities?" | "Economic opportunity remains the principal driver of rural-to-urban migration." |

### Developing the idea

Development is where Band 6 becomes Band 7. Four techniques:

1. **Causal chain.** Push each statement one step further. *"Free tuition widens access &rarr; more first-generation students enter university &rarr; the graduate workforce becomes more socially diverse &rarr; the economic benefits of education are distributed more evenly."* Four clauses, one idea, genuine depth.
2. **Concrete example.** "For instance, Finland's decision to abolish tuition fees was followed by a marked rise in enrolment among students from low-income households." You may invent plausible examples; they need not be verifiable, but they must be plausible and specific. Avoid invented statistics.
3. **Consequence.** "The result is that..." / "This in turn means that..." / "The implication for policymakers is that..."
4. **Counter and rebut.** "It might be objected that this imposes an unsustainable burden on the taxpayer. However, the fiscal cost is substantially offset by the higher lifetime tax contributions of graduates."

:::warn
**The listing trap.** "There are many advantages. Firstly, it saves money. Secondly, it saves time. Thirdly, it is convenient. Fourthly, it is popular." Four ideas, zero development, Band 5 for Task Response. **One idea, four sentences** beats **four ideas, four sentences** every single time.
:::

**Model body paragraph (Band 8).**

> "The most compelling argument for free higher education concerns social mobility. When tuition must be paid upfront or financed through substantial debt, the decision to attend university becomes a calculation about risk, and that calculation weighs far more heavily on students from low-income households than on their wealthier peers. A young person whose family has no experience of higher education, and no capacity to absorb the cost of failure, is therefore systematically less likely to apply, even when equally capable. Removing the financial barrier does not eliminate this disparity entirely, but it does remove its most concrete component. The experience of countries such as Germany, which charges no tuition at public universities, suggests that participation rates among first-generation students respond measurably to the removal of fees. In this sense, free education functions less as a subsidy than as a corrective to a market that under-serves talent."

*One idea. Topic sentence, mechanism explained across three sentences, a specific example, a concluding interpretation. 145 words — slightly long, but it shows what full development looks like.*

## 6.6 Coherence and cohesion

This criterion is 25% of the Writing band, and it is the one candidates address most crudely — by sprinkling linking words.

:::key
**Coherence** is logical organisation: ideas in a sensible order, one paragraph per idea, a clear line of argument from introduction to conclusion.
**Cohesion** is surface linkage: the words and grammar that connect sentences — conjunctions, reference words, substitution, repetition of key terms.
You need both. Perfect cohesion in an illogical essay scores badly; perfect logic without linkage reads as a list.
:::

### The linking-word bank

| Function | Formal linkers |
| --- | --- |
| **Adding** | Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Additionally, What is more, Besides this |
| **Contrasting** | However, Nevertheless, Nonetheless, Conversely, On the other hand, By contrast, That said |
| **Conceding** | Although, Even though, While, Whereas, Admittedly, It is true that, Granted |
| **Causing** | Because, Since, As, Owing to, Due to, On account of |
| **Resulting** | Therefore, Consequently, As a result, Thus, Hence, Accordingly, For this reason |
| **Exemplifying** | For instance, For example, To illustrate, A case in point is, Such as, Namely |
| **Sequencing** | To begin with, Subsequently, Following this, Finally |
| **Emphasising** | Indeed, In particular, Notably, Above all, Crucially |
| **Qualifying** | To some extent, In certain respects, Arguably, Broadly speaking |
| **Concluding** | In conclusion, To conclude, In summary, On balance, All things considered |

:::warn
**Overusing linkers is penalised.** The descriptors specifically mark down "mechanical" use. A linker at the start of every sentence is mechanical. Aim for roughly one linking device per two to three sentences, and vary the position: *"The policy, however, has proved difficult to implement"* is better than a fourth sentence-initial "However."
Also: **"Firstly, Secondly, Thirdly, Finally"** used as the skeleton of an essay is a Band 6 signature. Use them sparingly, inside a paragraph, not as your structure.
:::

### Cohesion beyond linking words

The techniques that actually distinguish Band 7 from Band 6:

| Device | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Reference words** | "Governments have introduced congestion charging. **This measure** has proved effective..." |
| **Substitution** | "Some cities have banned cars from the centre; **others** have simply raised parking charges." |
| **Ellipsis** | "Public transport receives generous funding in northern Europe, and cycling infrastructure **too**." |
| **Lexical chains** | *congestion &rarr; traffic &rarr; gridlock &rarr; vehicle volumes* — the same theme in varied words |
| **Parallel structure** | "Not only does it reduce emissions, but it also improves public health." |
| **Old-to-new information flow** | End each sentence with new information; begin the next with it. |

The last of these is the invisible engine of good academic prose. Compare:

> **Choppy:** "Congestion charging reduces traffic. Air quality improves because of fewer cars. Cities like London have this system."
> **Cohesive:** "Congestion charging reduces the volume of traffic entering city centres. **That reduction** translates directly into improved air quality, since fewer vehicles mean lower emissions at street level. **London**, which has operated such a scheme since 2003, offers the clearest evidence of this effect."

## 6.7 The conclusion

Two to three sentences. It must:
1. **Restate your position** in different words from the introduction.
2. **Summarise your two main reasons** in compressed form.
3. Optionally, add a **final thought** — a recommendation or an implication.

It must **not**: introduce a new idea; contradict the introduction; ask a rhetorical question; apologise for the essay's brevity.

**Conclusion frames:**
- *In conclusion, while [concession], the evidence points clearly towards [position], principally because [reason 1] and [reason 2].*
- *On balance, therefore, I would maintain that [position]. [Final thought].*
- *To summarise, [restated thesis]. Unless [condition], [implication].*

**Worked conclusion** (for the internet essay above):

> "In conclusion, although the internet is capable of substituting shallow interaction for genuine intimacy, the evidence of the past two decades suggests that it more often extends the reach of existing relationships than replaces them. The decisive factor is not the technology itself but the use to which it is put."

## 6.8 Grammatical range, lexical resource, and the mistakes that cap you

Chapters 9 and 10 treat these fully. Two points belong here because they are specifically essay-level.

**Sentence variety is not optional.** The teacher takes an entire session on it: simple, compound and complex sentences, and the explicit warning that an essay of simple sentences "will not give you more than Band 5". A Band 7 paragraph typically contains a short simple sentence for emphasis, one or two compound sentences, and two or three complex sentences with subordinate clauses.

**Precision beats obscurity in vocabulary.** Reaching for rare words you do not control produces collocation errors that damage Lexical Resource more than plain accurate language would. "A plethora of detrimental ramifications" is worse than "a range of harmful effects" if you cannot use *plethora* correctly.

## 6.9 Planning: the five minutes that decide the essay

:::key
**The five-minute plan**
**Minute 1.** Underline the task sentence. Identify the question type. Write it down: "DISCUSSION + OPINION".
**Minute 2.** Decide your position. Write it as one sentence. This is your thesis.
**Minutes 3&ndash;4.** Write two topic sentences — one per body paragraph. Under each, note three words: the mechanism, the example, the consequence.
**Minute 5.** Check: does each topic sentence serve the thesis? Does the plan answer *every* part of the task?
Then write. Thirty minutes for roughly 280 words is nine words a minute — comfortable, if you are not also inventing the argument as you go.
:::

**A completed plan:**

> TASK: Do advantages of remote work outweigh disadvantages? &rarr; VERDICT REQUIRED
> THESIS: Yes, on balance — autonomy + access, outweigh isolation
> BP1: Autonomy raises productivity &rarr; no commute, control over schedule &rarr; e.g. tech firms reporting output gains &rarr; therefore employers benefit too
> BP2: Widens labour market access &rarr; carers, disabled workers, rural residents &rarr; e.g. rural graduates no longer forced to relocate &rarr; social as well as economic gain
> CONCEDE (in BP2 or intro): isolation and blurred boundaries are real
> CONCLUSION: benefits structural, drawbacks manageable with policy

## 6.10 Twenty essay topics to prepare

Task 2 topics recur within a limited set of domains. Prepare ideas — not memorised essays — for each.

| Domain | Typical questions |
| --- | --- |
| **Education** | Free university; single-sex schools; the value of arts subjects; homework; exams vs continuous assessment; studying abroad |
| **Technology** | Social media and isolation; AI and employment; screen time and children; online vs classroom learning |
| **Environment** | Individual vs government responsibility; plastic; renewable energy costs; protecting endangered species; carbon taxes |
| **Work** | Remote working; work-life balance; job security; retirement age; gig economy |
| **Society** | Ageing populations; urbanisation; crime and punishment; equality; immigration |
| **Health** | Public vs private healthcare; junk food advertising; sport funding; mental health |
| **Culture** | Globalisation and local traditions; language extinction; tourism; museums |
| **Government** | Public spending priorities; censorship; taxation; space exploration funding |

For each, write down three arguments for, three against, and one concrete example. That preparation — about four hours of work — means you will never face a Task 2 prompt with nothing to say.

## 6.11 A complete model essay (Band 8&ndash;9)

> **Prompt:** "In many countries, the gap between the rich and the poor is widening. What problems does this cause, and what measures could be taken to address it?"

Rising economic inequality has become one of the defining features of the contemporary world, and its consequences extend well beyond the balance sheets of individual households. This essay will argue that the most serious problems it generates are the erosion of social cohesion and the entrenchment of disadvantage across generations, and that progressive fiscal policy combined with sustained investment in public services offers the most realistic response.

The most corrosive effect of widening inequality is the gradual dissolution of the sense that a society is a shared enterprise. When the material circumstances of the wealthiest and the poorest diverge sufficiently, the two groups cease to use the same schools, hospitals, transport networks and public spaces, and with that separation the willingness to fund collective provision declines. A citizen who has never used a public hospital has little incentive to vote for its funding. This dynamic is self-reinforcing: as public services deteriorate, those who can afford alternatives withdraw from them, which further reduces political support for investment. The outcome is not merely inequality of income but inequality of citizenship, and it manifests in the political polarisation and declining institutional trust visible across much of the developed world.

A second and equally intractable problem is that extreme inequality tends to become hereditary. Educational attainment correlates strongly with parental income, not primarily because of innate differences but because wealthier families can purchase tutoring, housing in better catchment areas, and the freedom for their children to take unpaid internships or pursue extended study. Poverty, conversely, imposes costs that compound: insecure housing disrupts schooling, and the absence of a financial cushion forces early entry into low-paid work. Within two generations, what began as a difference in income becomes a difference in opportunity that no amount of individual effort reliably overcomes.

Addressing this requires action on both the distribution of income and the provision of services. On the fiscal side, genuinely progressive taxation — particularly of inherited wealth and unearned capital gains, which are frequently taxed more lightly than wages — would slow the accumulation of advantage at the top while generating revenue. On the expenditure side, that revenue should be directed towards the services that most powerfully equalise opportunity: early-years education, where the returns to intervention are highest; accessible healthcare; and affordable housing in areas with employment. The Nordic countries demonstrate that such an approach is compatible with economic competitiveness, having maintained comparatively low levels of inequality without sacrificing growth.

In conclusion, the widening gap between rich and poor damages societies by fracturing social solidarity and by converting temporary disadvantage into permanent exclusion. Neither problem is self-correcting, but both are susceptible to deliberate policy, and a combination of progressive taxation and well-targeted public investment represents the most credible path towards a fairer distribution of opportunity.

*448 words — longer than you should write under exam conditions, and offered here as a demonstration of what full development looks like. In the exam, compress each body paragraph by a third.*

## 6.12 The Task 2 checklist

- I identified the question type and answered **every** part of the task.
- Four (or five) paragraphs, clearly separated.
- Introduction paraphrases the prompt — no copied phrases.
- A thesis statement with a clear position.
- Each body paragraph opens with a topic sentence expressing one idea.
- Each body paragraph explains, exemplifies and links.
- My position in the conclusion matches my position in the introduction.
- No new ideas in the conclusion.
- Linkers varied, not mechanical, not sentence-initial every time.
- A mixture of simple, compound and complex sentences.
- No contractions, no bullet points, no headings, no idioms, no invented statistics.
- Between 260 and 300 words.
- Five minutes spent checking articles, plurals, subject-verb agreement and tense.

## 6.13 Practice tasks

**6A (Opinion).** *Some people believe that governments should invest in public transport rather than building new roads. To what extent do you agree or disagree?*

**6B (Discussion).** *Some argue that children benefit from having a wide range of activities outside school, while others believe unstructured free time is more valuable. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.*

**6C (Problem/Solution).** *An increasing number of people in cities are living alone. What are the causes of this trend, and what effects does it have on society?*

**6D (Advantages/Disadvantages).** *Many companies now monitor their employees' activity electronically. Do the advantages of this practice outweigh the disadvantages?*

**6E (Two-part).** *Fewer young people are choosing to study science at university. Why might this be? What could be done to reverse the trend?*

Forty minutes each. Then rewrite each one with no time limit, consulting the checklist. Compare the two versions: the difference between them is precisely the work you need to make automatic.
