# Chapter 1: Understanding the IELTS Test

## 1.1 What IELTS Is — and What It Is Not

The International English Language Testing System (IELTS) is the world's most widely taken English proficiency test for study, work, and migration. It is jointly owned by the British Council, IDP: IELTS Australia, and Cambridge University Press and Assessment, and it is accepted by more than 12,000 organisations worldwide, including universities, employers, professional bodies, and immigration authorities in the UK, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the USA.

IELTS is a test of **language skill, not subject knowledge**. As the video course stresses repeatedly (Video Parts 1–3), the Reading test examines your reading *skills* — not what you know about the topic — and the same principle applies everywhere: examiners reward how you use English, never the opinions you express. There are no "right" or "wrong" ideas in Speaking and Writing; there is only effective or ineffective communication.

> IELTS tests four separate skills — Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking — and reports each one on a 9-band scale. Your Overall Band Score is the average of the four, rounded to the nearest half band.

## 1.2 The Two Test Formats: Academic vs General Training

Everyone takes the same Listening and Speaking tests. Reading and Writing differ by module:

| Feature | Academic | General Training |
|---|---|---|
| Who it is for | University study (undergraduate/postgraduate), professional registration (doctors, nurses, engineers) | Migration, work experience, training, secondary education |
| Reading texts | 3 long texts from books, journals, magazines, newspapers; academic topics of general interest for a non-specialist audience | 3–5 shorter texts: notices, adverts, timetables, company handbooks, official documents, plus one longer general-interest text |
| Writing Task 1 | Describe visual information (graph, table, chart, process, map) in at least 150 words | Write a letter (formal, semi-formal, or informal) in at least 150 words |
| Writing Task 2 | Same for both: discursive essay of at least 250 words | Same for both: discursive essay of at least 250 words |
| Listening | Identical: 4 sections, 40 questions, 30 minutes (+10 minutes transfer on paper) | Identical |
| Speaking | Identical: 3 parts, 11–14 minutes, face-to-face or video-call with an examiner | Identical |

! Choose your module before you book. Universities almost always require Academic; immigration usually requires General Training. If you take the wrong module, your result is useless for your purpose and there are no refunds.

## 1.3 Paper-Based vs Computer-Delivered IELTS

Content, timing, scoring, and question types are identical. Only the delivery differs:

| Feature | Paper-based | Computer-delivered |
|---|---|---|
| Listening transfer time | 10 minutes to copy answers to the answer sheet | 2 minutes to check answers on screen |
| Reading/Writing | Booklets + answer sheets; handwritten essays | On-screen reading with highlighting; typed essays with word counter |
| Results | 3–5 days (faster than the old 13-day wait in most centres) | 1–2 days |
| Speaking | Face-to-face (unchanged in both options) | Face-to-face or video call, depending on centre |
| Best for | Candidates who think on paper, sketch plans by hand, or type slowly | Fast typists, candidates who want quick results and easy editing |

> The video course (Part 1) demonstrates the paper test with its CD-based Listening. Every strategy in this book works for both delivery modes; differences are flagged wherever they matter (notably Listening transfer time and Writing word counts).

## 1.4 The Four Modules at a Glance

### Listening — 30 minutes, 4 sections, 40 questions

| Section | Context | Typical speakers | Typical tasks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Everyday social situation (booking, enquiry, application) | 2 speakers: conversation | Form, note, table completion (e.g., the course's *Dubai Palm Apartments* enquiry) |
| 2 | Everyday social situation (monologue: tour, talk, instructions) | 1 speaker | Map labelling, matching, multiple choice, sentence completion |
| 3 | Academic/training discussion (students + tutor) | 2–4 speakers | Multiple choice, matching, diagram/flow-chart completion (e.g., the course's *Self-Access Centre* discussion) |
| 4 | Academic lecture/monologue | 1 speaker | Note/summary completion, table completion, short answers |

Each correct answer earns one mark; the raw score out of 40 converts to a band (see 1.6). Spelling must be correct. If the instruction says **NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS**, writing three words makes the answer wrong even if the meaning is right.

### Reading — 60 minutes, 3 sections, 40 questions

Academic: 3 passages totalling 2,200–2,750 words, increasing in difficulty. General Training: Section 1 (2–3 short everyday texts), Section 2 (2 workplace texts), Section 3 (1 long general-interest text). There is **no extra transfer time** — you must write answers on the answer sheet as you go (paper) or as you read (computer).

The course's golden rule (Parts 2–3): **IELTS Reading is a test of understanding synonym language.** Questions paraphrase the passage; answers are found by matching *meaning*, never identical words. You also do **not** have to answer questions 1–40 in order — Chapters 6–8 teach you the optimal order per passage.

### Writing — 60 minutes, 2 tasks

| Task | Academic | General Training | Time | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Task 1 (150+ words) | Report describing a graph, table, chart, process/cycle, or map | Letter for a given situation and purpose | ~20 min | 1/3 of Writing band |
| Task 2 (250+ words) | Essay responding to a point of view, argument, or problem | Identical to Academic | ~40 min | 2/3 of Writing band |

You are scored in four equally weighted criteria (25% each): Task Achievement (T1) / Task Response (T2), Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Chapter 11 unpacks every criterion with the exact descriptors examiners use.

### Speaking — 11–14 minutes, 3 parts, with a human examiner

| Part | Format | Timing | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Interview: familiar topics (home, work, study, hobbies) | 4–5 min | Short questions, short answers (2–4 sentences each). Examiners listen for natural tense control (see the course's *Using the right verb tense* lesson, Part 5). |
| 2 | Long turn: 1-minute preparation + 1–2 minute talk from a cue card | 3–4 min | You speak alone. Structure + past-tense storytelling + opinion = high bands. |
| 3 | Discussion: abstract questions linked to Part 2 | 4–5 min | Longer, analytical answers with reasons, examples, and concessions. |

Scoring mirrors Writing: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation — 25% each. There is no pass or fail: you are placed on the 9-band scale.

## 1.6 Scoring: Raw Marks to Bands

Listening and Reading bands come from raw scores. The conversion varies slightly per test version; these are the standard boundaries:

| Band | Listening (raw /40) | Reading Academic (raw /40) | Reading GT (raw /40) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | 39–40 | 39–40 | 40 |
| 8.5 | 37–38 | 37–38 | 39 |
| 8 | 35–36 | 35–36 | 37–38 |
| 7.5 | 32–34 | 33–34 | 35–36 |
| 7 | 30–31 | 30–32 | 33–34 |
| 6.5 | 26–29 | 27–29 | 31–32 |
| 6 | 23–25 | 23–26 | 28–30 |
| 5.5 | 19–22 | 19–22 | 24–27 |
| 5 | 15–18 | 15–18 | 20–23 |
| 4.5 | 13–14 | 13–14 | 17–19 |
| 4 | 10–12 | 10–12 | 14–16 |

Writing and Speaking are scored directly against public band descriptors (Chapters 9 and 11 reproduce the working versions). The Overall Band is the mean of the four module bands, rounded: anything ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band; anything ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band. So 6.25 becomes 6.5, 6.75 becomes 7.0, and 6.125 becomes 6.0.

### What each band means (official summary)

| Band | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | Expert user | Full operational command; accurate, fluent, fully appropriate English. |
| 8 | Very good user | Fully operational command with only occasional unsystematic inaccuracies. |
| 7 | Good user | Operational command with occasional inaccuracies and misunderstandings in some situations. |
| 6 | Competent user | Effective command despite inaccuracies and misunderstandings; can use fairly complex language in familiar situations. |
| 5 | Modest user | Partial command; copes with overall meaning in most situations but makes many mistakes. |
| 4 | Limited user | Basic competence limited to familiar situations; frequent problems in understanding and expression. |
| 0–3 | Extremely limited / intermittent / non-user | Little or no communicative ability. |

Most universities require 6.5–7.5 overall (often with minimum module scores); professional registration commonly demands 7.0 in every module; migration thresholds vary by country and visa class. Check your target institution's exact requirement — including minimum per-module scores — before you plan your preparation.

## 1.7 Frequently Asked Questions

**How long is my result valid?** Two years from the test date. After that, organisations will not accept it.

**Can I retake?** Yes, as often as you like, including One Skill Retake (available in many centres): if you miss your target in exactly one module, you can resit just that module within 60 days of the original test.

**Can I ask for a remark?** Yes — an Enquiry on Results (EOR) re-marks your test. It takes 2–21 days and the fee is refunded if your score changes. Remarks most often succeed in Writing and Speaking (±0.5).

**British or American spelling?** Either is accepted everywhere, but be consistent within Writing, and in Listening/Reading match the recording's or passage's spelling where a word is given.

**Is IELTS easier in some countries?** No. The test, scoring, and examiner standards are global. Choose the centre where you feel most comfortable and can get your preferred date.

**How much do I need to write?** Task 1: at least 150 words (aim 160–190). Task 2: at least 250 words (aim 260–290). Under-length answers lose Task Achievement/Response marks automatically. There is no maximum, but overlong answers waste time and accumulate errors.

**Can I use a pen or must I use a pencil?** On paper, use a pencil (HB) for Listening and Reading answer sheets — you can erase and correct. For Writing, pen or pencil are both accepted; pencil is safer for corrections.

**What if I miss my Speaking test?** It may be scheduled on a different day from the other modules (up to 7 days before or after). Confirm your Speaking slot and arrive 30 minutes early.

## 1.8 Booking, ID & Test-Day Rules

**Booking (4 steps):** 1. Choose module (Academic/GT) + format (paper/computer) + centre at ielts.org. 2. Pick a date at least 6–8 weeks out (popular centres fill up). 3. Register with the EXACT name on your ID (passport/national ID); mismatches bar entry. 4. Pay the fee (varies by country, roughly $200–300 USD equivalent).

**ID rules:** bring the same original, valid, photographic ID used at registration — no photocopies, no expired documents. Some centres photograph/fingerprint you on arrival; this is normal.

**What happens on the day (paper):** morning check-in → Listening (~40 min with transfer) → Reading (60 min) → Writing (60 min), back-to-back with short supervised breaks. Speaking may be the same afternoon or within ±7 days — confirm your slot.

**Prohibited:** phones/smartwatches (switched off + stored), notes, dictionaries, food in the room (water in clear bottles usually allowed), talking during modules. Cheating (including impersonation and answer-sharing) brings bans up to 2 years and result cancellation.

**Special arrangements:** dyslexia, hearing/vision needs, medical conditions — contact your centre 6+ weeks ahead with documentation. Extra time, Braille, lip-reading versions, and separate rooms are available.
